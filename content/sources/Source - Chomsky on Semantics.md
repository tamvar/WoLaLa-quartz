## Summary

Michael Glanzberg explains why the claim that Chomsky rejects semantics is both understandable and seriously misleading. Meaning has always supplied important data for generative linguistics, and the relation between syntax and interpretation has remained part of grammatical theory. Chomsky's skepticism instead targets particular semantic architectures: generative semantics, reductions of syntax to meaning, and especially reference-first truth-conditional semantics.

Glanzberg reconstructs a stringent Chomskyan possibility in which much of what is called semantics belongs to syntactic computation oriented toward a conceptual–intentional interface rather than to a separate semantic module. He also makes clear that this reconstruction is controversial. Many generative semanticists accept internalist cognitive explanation while retaining compositional semantic values and truth conditions. The chapter maps a live dispute rather than closing it in Chomsky's favor.

## Meaning as Evidence Without Reduction

The autonomy of syntax does not mean that semantic evidence is irrelevant. Ambiguity, anomaly, scope, and interpretation can reveal that superficially similar strings have different structures. Chomsky's well-known examples show both that grammatical form can be investigated independently of plausibility and that interpretive contrasts can motivate syntactic analysis.

The autonomy claim is explanatory: syntactic categories, relations, and operations should not simply be derived from meanings or communicative functions. A semantic contrast may provide evidence for two structures without making those structures semantic objects. This distinction separates the use of meaning as data from the reduction of syntax to semantics.

## Changing Syntax–Semantics Interfaces

Glanzberg traces several stages in generative theory:

- In the *Aspects* framework, deep structure supplied input to semantic interpretation, while transformations led toward surface form and phonetic interpretation.
- Katz, Fodor, and Postal proposed semantic markers and projection rules, often with the assumption that transformations preserve meaning.
- Scope and passive constructions strained simple meaning-preservation claims.
- Generative semantics pushed deep structure toward a logical or semantic representation and treated transformations as connecting that representation to surface form.
- Interpretive semantics, associated with Chomsky and Jackendoff, allowed semantic interpretation to draw on more than one syntactic level rather than identifying syntax with semantics.
- Later work developed Logical Form, and Minimalism recast the grammar as a computational system whose outputs must be legible to sensorimotor and conceptual–intentional systems.

Across these changes, the interface moves and its representational vocabulary changes. The persistent commitment is that syntax supplies structured input to interpretation without being derived from a prior semantic representation.

## A Strict Chomskyan Reconstruction

Glanzberg considers a strong interpretation of the Minimalist picture. Lexical items are bundles of features, including features associated with animacy, artifacthood, or substance. The same combinatorial system that constructs syntactic objects organizes material for the conceptual–intentional interface. On this view, the internal semantic contribution of I-language may be largely syntactic computation under another description.

This is a reconstruction, not an uncontested statement of consensus. It raises questions about whether there is a separate semantics module, how much content is fixed within I-language, and what happens at the conceptual–intentional interface. A weak internal semantics may constrain full content without determining it.

The chapter therefore distinguishes at least three claims:

- syntax is autonomous and is not derived from meaning;
- grammar produces representations legible to conceptual systems;
- there is no scientifically useful semantic component beyond syntax.

The first is a central Chomskyan commitment. The second belongs to the interface architecture. The third is a stronger and more controversial extrapolation.

## The Truth-Conditional Target

Truth-conditional semantics characteristically assigns semantic values to lexical expressions and explains how composition determines sentence truth conditions. Chomsky's objections begin with natural-language expressions that do not appear to have stable referents or extensions. Words such as `book` shift across physical, informational, and institutional aspects; ordinary names and descriptions are similarly sensitive to context and interest.

The skepticism is foundational rather than merely technical. If reference is interest-relative and ordinary lexical items lack stable extensions, a reference-first account may misdescribe the phenomena that a theory of human language should explain. Chomsky also doubts that abstract semantic values explain communication better than an account of agents using internal linguistic resources in particular circumstances.

Glanzberg emphasizes that this target is narrower than meaning as such. Chomsky recognizes semantic facts, lexical features, interpretive relations, and conceptual systems. He disputes whether truth conditions and reference supply the right explanatory basis.

## Replies from Generative Semantics

The chapter does not leave Chomsky's objections unanswered. Work by Higginbotham, Partee, Ludlow, Kennedy and Stanley, King, and others shows that truth-conditional methods can generate detailed empirical explanations. Internalist psychology also need not prohibit relational properties. A cognitive system can be individuated internally while its states participate in external reference relations, or external relations can figure in explanations at another level.

These responses undermine any simple inference from methodological naturalism or individual psychology to the rejection of semantic values. Formal semantic analyses may earn their place through explanatory success even if no lexical item has a context-invariant everyday extension. Context sensitivity can be modeled rather than treated as fatal to the program.

Glanzberg accordingly presents the dispute as unresolved. Chomsky defends a strong autonomy thesis and challenges the explanatory standing of truth-conditional semantics; opponents argue that formal semantics supplies genuine generalizations and can coexist with cognitive science.

## What Chomsky Rejects—and What He Does Not

The chapter supports a precise summary:

- Chomsky rejects generative semantics when it makes syntax derivative from semantic or logical structure.
- He is skeptical of truth-conditional semantics as a foundational theory of natural-language meaning.
- He preserves the autonomy of syntax and an internalist conception of language.
- He treats meaning and interpretation as central sources of evidence.
- He permits lexical and interface features connected to conceptual systems.
- He does not thereby provide a complete positive theory of all semantic phenomena.

Saying simply that Chomsky `has no semantics` erases these distinctions. Saying that his framework includes ordinary formal semantics is equally inaccurate.

## Relation to Language Models

The chapter predates no relevant model results and offers no direct LLM theory. Its value is architectural. Model research often infers a semantic theory from behavior without distinguishing the autonomy of learned structure, the existence of an internal interface representation, and reference or truth conditions.

An LLM may encode features useful for semantic judgments without implementing a truth-conditional semantics. Conversely, success on truth-evaluable tasks may not show that syntactic organization is derived from meaning. Probes or behavioral tests can establish sensitivity to semantic distinctions while leaving the explanatory architecture open.

The chapter also cautions against treating internalism as a shortcut to semantic conclusions. Showing that a representation is internal does not establish its content, and showing that reference is relational does not exclude internal computational explanation. Evidence must identify which semantic claim and explanatory level are at issue.

## Limitations and Interpretive Cautions

- Glanzberg's strict syntax-centered account is explicitly a reconstruction whose status is disputed.
- The historical map compresses several internally diverse research programs.
- Chomsky's objections challenge the foundations of truth-conditional semantics but do not constitute a decisive empirical refutation of all formal-semantic work.
- The chapter reports replies that preserve internalism while allowing external relations; it should not be cited as a one-sided defense of Chomsky.
- The language-model implications are interpretive extensions beyond the chapter.
