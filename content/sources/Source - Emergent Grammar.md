## Summary
Paul Hopper's 1987 paper presents grammar as temporal, provisional, negotiable, and continually emerging in discourse rather than as a complete rule system that speakers possess prior to use. Regularity arises through repetition, formulaicity, prior textuality, recurrent figures, and ongoing grammaticization. Discourse is therefore not merely the performance of an independently given grammar: it is where speakers reuse, negotiate, and extend patterns that are always moving toward structure. Hopper does not deny that stable regularities exist. He challenges the explanatory move from the linguist's grammatical description to an abstract, prior, mentally represented system treated as the cause of those regularities.

## Key Points

- Grammar is emergent and processual: its regularities are continually produced and revised through discourse.
- Repetition and formulaicity make prior texts available as resources for new utterances; recurrent figures and preferred clause units supply local organization without requiring a complete prior system.
- Grammaticization is ongoing rather than merely an account of remote historical origins. Hopper's slogan is that there is “no grammar but grammaticization.”
- Stable and widespread patterns are compatible with emergence. The dispute concerns their explanatory source, not their existence.
- Hopper's target is specifically an abstract, prior, mentally represented rule system inferred from the linguist's model—not abstraction in every possible sense.
- The paper questions arguments that move from a useful grammatical description to the claim that speakers possess that description as an autonomous competence.

## Evidence and Examples

Hopper's discussion of English *a/an* connects grammatical form with repeated discourse functions and historical development. The article is not offered merely as a genealogy of an already completed rule. It illustrates the continuing movement from recurrent use toward structural regularity.

Prefabricated discourse and prior textuality provide a second line of evidence. Utterances regularly reuse formulae, familiar fragments, and earlier textual patterns. Preferred clause units and recurrent figures show how discourse can be organized around repeatedly available configurations rather than generated afresh from a fully specified prior grammar. Hopper's Old English examples make this visible in textual patterns whose regularity depends on recurrent figures and discourse organization.

The Malay material traces ergative constructions to serial-verb patterns and discourse conditions. It supplies a language-specific case in which a construction becomes more grammatical through repeated use and reanalysis. Together, these examples motivate Hopper's formulation that there is “no grammar but grammaticization”: structure is always emerging, never simply absent, but never finally detached from the processes that sustain it.

The concluding competence syllogisms identify a methodological slippage. A linguist constructs a grammar that models patterns in a body of speech; the model is then attributed to speakers as a mentally represented system; observed language is finally explained as the output of that system. Hopper challenges the move from successful description to prior mental possession. The grammar may be a useful model without being an autonomous object that exists before discourse and determines it.

## Interpretation

Hopper's argument supports a use-based alternative to rule-first or strongly autonomous accounts of grammar. Structure remains real and consequential, but its stability is explained through recurrent practice, negotiation, formulaicity, and grammaticization. This is stronger than the claim that surface frequencies matter and narrower than a denial of every form of abstraction.

The connection to language models is interpretive rather than source-internal. Distributional learning and large-scale prediction make it reasonable to ask whether some grammatical organization can arise from recurrent use without a fully prespecified grammar. Hopper's paper does not show that contemporary language models acquire the relevant kind of structure, that their training resembles human discourse, or that behavioral success settles the competence question. It instead clarifies the theoretical position against which such claims must be evaluated.

## WoLaLa Relevance

The source is especially useful for:

- distinguishing ongoing emergence from a one-time historical origin story;
- separating grammaticization, formulaicity, and discourse negotiation from generic appeals to frequency;
- identifying the inference from a linguist's model to a mentally represented competence as a substantive theoretical move;
- framing language-model comparisons without treating distributional success as automatic confirmation of emergent grammar.

## Limitation Or Open Question

This is a programmatic theoretical paper, not a direct assessment of contemporary models. Its examples motivate an explanatory stance but do not by themselves determine which learning mechanisms recover grammatical structure, how stable generalizations should be represented, or whether modern neural systems instantiate Hopper's account rather than approximate selected grammatical behaviors.
