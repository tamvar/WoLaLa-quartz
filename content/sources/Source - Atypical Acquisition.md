## Summary

Neil Smith and Ianthi Tsimpli use atypical language acquisition to investigate the contributions of input, critical periods, linguistic endowment, and other cognitive systems. They distinguish learners whose linguistic input is absent, reduced, or inaccessible from learners whose neurocognitive profiles selectively affect language or communication. Across deprivation, developmental language disorder, autism, and the polyglot savant Christopher, the chapter argues for partial autonomy among formal language, general intelligence, and communicative ability.

## Two Sources of Atypicality

The first class involves restricted access during development. Cases include extreme social isolation, children exposed only to incomplete systems, deaf children without accessible signed input, and deaf-blind children. Genie’s limited later acquisition supports a sensitive-period effect, though trauma and deprivation prevent a clean language-only inference. Deafness itself is not a linguistic impairment: children with natural signed input can acquire language normally. The decisive variable is timely access.

Nicaraguan Sign Language and homesign show learners imposing organization on limited input, but they do not imply that ordinary language develops in complete isolation. Community transmission and successive cohorts matter. The cases demonstrate both the learner’s structuring contribution and the necessity of usable linguistic experience.

The second class involves neurocognitive differences despite plentiful input. Developmental Language Disorder primarily affects aspects of language; autism more often involves heterogeneous language profiles alongside characteristic communicative and pragmatic differences. These patterns allow distinctions among formal grammar, interfaces, and broader social cognition to be tested rather than treating “language ability” as unitary.

## Christopher and Dissociation

Christopher learned vocabulary and grammatical patterns in many languages despite severe limitations in nonverbal cognition and everyday communication. His translation failures could persist even when he knew the relevant lexical distinction, suggesting that word recognition and formal linguistic knowledge can dissociate from communicatively appropriate use.

Artificial-language experiments provide a stronger architectural probe. Christopher could learn linguistically plausible patterns but not structure-independent rules requiring counting; control participants could solve analogous counting puzzles outside a language-learning setting yet also failed to learn them as language. The authors interpret this as evidence that linguistic hypothesis formation is constrained differently from general problem solving.

## WoLaLa Interpretation

The chapter warns against collapsing competence, task success, communication, and general intelligence into one scale. Language models may show strong formal performance without grounded communicative competence, or fail a task because of interface and prompting demands rather than absence of the target representation. Dissociation evidence should motivate component-level tests.

Human atypical cases are not direct analogies for model ablations. Their value is methodological: apparent co-occurrence does not establish a single mechanism, and selective strengths or impairments can reveal architecture only when input history, access, task, and alternative capacities are carefully controlled.

## Limitations

- Many deprivation cases contain unavoidable medical, social, emotional, and educational confounds.
- Diagnostic categories such as autism and DLD are internally heterogeneous.
- Single-case dissociations are theoretically informative but require caution in generalization.
- The chapter favors a modular and nativist interpretation without exhaustively testing alternatives.
