## Summary
*Mind Design II: Philosophy, Psychology, and Artificial Intelligence*, edited by John Haugeland, is a historically central source container for classic disputes about intelligence, intentionality, symbolic search, knowledge representation, connectionism, and the limits of formal AI. This note treats the volume selectively rather than as a monolithic argument. Its WoLaLa value lies in showing the major fault lines that still structure current LLM debates: Turing-style behavioral criteria, Dennett's intentional stance, symbolic search programs, Dreyfus-style critiques, Searle's anti-strong-AI argument, and connectionist alternatives.

## Selective Scope
This source page treats the collection selectively as a historical anchor for:

- classic strong-AI and anti-strong-AI disputes
- symbolic search and knowledge-representation programs
- intentionality and the intentional stance
- connectionist alternatives to GOFAI
- the framing question of what it would mean to design a mind

## Key Points
- The volume is a curated confrontation among several major schools rather than a unified doctrine.
- Haugeland's introduction frames AI as a problem of how matter could count as minded under the right organization and perspective.
- The contents juxtapose Turing, Dennett, Newell and Simon, Minsky, Dreyfus, Searle, Rumelhart, and Smolensky, making the classic disputes unusually visible in one place.
- The collection is valuable for WoLaLa because many present arguments about language models replay these same disagreements under new empirical conditions.
- It is especially important as a bridge between symbolic AI history and later connectionist views, rather than as a source on language alone.

## Details
The visible table of contents makes the relevance of the volume much clearer than earlier planning notes had allowed. The collection opens with Haugeland's framing essay, then includes Turing's "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," Dennett's "True Believers," Newell and Simon on symbols and search, Minsky on knowledge representation, Dreyfus on AI at an impasse, Searle's "Minds, Brains, and Programs," Rumelhart on connectionism, and Smolensky on connectionist modeling.

That sequence is useful because it lays out the core alternatives with unusual directness. The symbolic tradition treats intelligence as computation over explicit structures, often search-guided and representationally articulated. Dreyfus and Searle challenge different parts of that story: one by pressing embodiment, skill, and situated practice, the other by arguing that formal symbol manipulation is insufficient for understanding. Connectionist entries then provide a route away from classical symbolic pictures without surrendering the hope of mechanistic explanation.

For WoLaLa, the source matters because current language-model debates often smuggle in one of these positions without naming it. Claims that LLMs are just next-token predictors often echo older worries about syntax without semantics. Claims that capability should be judged behaviorally often sit closer to Turing and Dennett. Claims that distributed systems learn important structure without explicit symbolic encoding echo the connectionist side. The collection therefore functions as a durable map of recurrent argumentative positions.
