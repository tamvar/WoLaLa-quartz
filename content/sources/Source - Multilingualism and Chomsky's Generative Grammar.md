## Summary

Tanja Kupisch, Sergio Miguel Pereira Soares, Eloi Puig-Mayenco, and Jason Rothman argue that generative grammar (GG) is compatible with, and productively constrained by, multilingual evidence. GG is principally a theory of possible attained grammatical states, not a developmental theory by itself. Yet its representations and distinctions generate acquisition hypotheses. The chapter uses bilingual parameter setting and third-language transfer to show how multilingual research tests claims about grammatical knowledge while forcing careful separation of I-language representations from performance and contextual effects.

## Grammar and Acquisition

The authors reject the claim that GG’s use of an idealized speaker-hearer makes multilingualism irrelevant. An idealization isolates variables; it does not deny that multilingual minds are common or theoretically important. GG characterizes mental grammatical representations, while acquisition theories explain how learners reach those states. Multilingual development supplies unusually informative cases because multiple grammars interact in one person under unequal input, dominance, age, and use conditions.

Child bilingualism sharpens poverty-of-stimulus questions. A bilingual child typically receives less input in each language than a matched monolingual, yet can acquire language-specific grammatical patterns with high success. The chapter reviews parameter-setting work in which children distinguish systems rather than constructing a single indiscriminate mixture. The authors interpret differences between grammatical and lexical learning as evidence that grammar acquisition draws on structured prior knowledge, while acknowledging contextual variability in outcomes.

## Transfer and Cross-Linguistic Influence

The chapter’s most useful methodological distinction concerns adult third-language acquisition. `Transfer` is defined as copying an I-language representation from a previously acquired language into the developing grammar. `Cross-linguistic influence` is broader: momentary performance can be affected by language activation, dominance, frequency, memory, task, and context without those effects constituting transferred grammatical representation.

This distinction imposes design constraints. Evidence collected near the beginning of L3 acquisition is better suited to identifying transfer because later performance reflects learning and accumulated interaction. Comprehension and representation-sensitive measures may be more diagnostic than production alone. In the review cited by the authors, many studies tested learners beyond the initial state or relied heavily on production, weakening claims specifically about transfer.

## WoLaLa Interpretation

The chapter cautions against inferring a model’s grammatical organization from multilingual output alone. Cross-lingual interference can arise from shared representations, tokenizer overlap, training imbalance, retrieval conditions, or decoding. An I-language-inspired claim about model transfer needs contrasts that isolate stable representational change from transient context effects.

It also provides a constructive research template: study multilingual models as systems in which controlled variation can reveal representational units. Useful tests should separate acquisition history, input amount, task language, prompt context, and output mode rather than treating “multilingual performance” as one variable.

## Limitations

- The chapter selects two illustrative domains rather than surveying multilingual acquisition comprehensively.
- Its transfer/CLI distinction is a proposed methodological discipline, not a universally accepted terminology.
- Poverty-of-stimulus conclusions depend on assumptions about the available input and alternative learning mechanisms.
- Connections to language models are interpretive; the chapter reports no neural-model experiments.
