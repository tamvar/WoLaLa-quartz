## Summary

Paul Pietroski reconstructs a positive conception of meaning from Chomsky's work while explaining why it resists reference-first and extension-first semantics. On this view, an I-language is an internal, individual, and intensional procedure that generates expressions connecting pronunciations with meanings. Syntactic structure constrains interpretation, lexical meanings contribute conditions on use and reference, and complex meanings are systematically assembled. None of this requires words to have stable extensions or sentences to determine truth conditions.

The chapter is therefore not evidence that Chomsky denies meaning or systematic semantic inquiry. Its narrower conclusion is that ordinary lexical meanings are poorly modeled as sets of objects and that reference should not be the scientific foundation of semantics. Pietroski's positive reconstruction is interpretive: it draws together Chomskyan arguments and develops them in an explicitly internalist direction rather than reporting a single canonical semantic theory stated by Chomsky.

## Meaning Was Part of the Explanandum

Pietroski rejects a familiar historical caricature in which early generative linguistics bracketed meaning in favor of strings and formal syntax. Chomsky's simplified descriptions of languages as sets of sentences made it possible to distinguish kinds of recursive procedure, but the motivating evidence repeatedly involved relations between pronunciation, syntactic structure, and interpretation.

Constructional ambiguity is a central case. A single pronounced string can correspond to two or three expressions with different meanings, while apparently comparable strings may lack the expected ambiguity. Restrictions on control, extraction, anaphora, and relative-clause interpretation show that these patterns are not arbitrary products of contextual imagination. A theory of grammar must explain why some readings are available and others are not.

Phrase markers or derivational structures can accordingly serve as partial proxies for expression meanings. They do not exhaust semantic content, but they encode distinctions needed to explain correlations among structure, pronunciation, and interpretation. The scientific target is a generative procedure that produces structured expressions, not a bare set of sound sequences.

## I-Language and Internalist Semantics

An I-language is internal to an individual, intensional in the sense of being a particular procedure rather than merely the extension it generates, and capable of producing an unbounded range of expressions. Pietroski presents grammar schematically as a lexicon together with constrained operations for combining lexical items. The resulting expressions provide instructions or resources for systems of thought and articulation.

This internalism does not imply that speakers never refer to things or that relations to the world are unreal. It rejects a different claim: that a public language made up of shared word–object relations is the right scientific starting point for explaining human linguistic competence. Acts of referring depend on speakers, circumstances, intentions, and other cognitive capacities. Those relations should not be projected back into the lexicon as fixed semantic values without further argument.

The distinction between antecedence and co-denotation illustrates the point. An expression can depend anaphorically on an antecedent even when neither expression denotes an existing object, while two expressions may pick out the same object without standing in an antecedence relation. Grammar tracks structural and interpretive dependencies that cannot be reduced to identity of worldly referents.

## Why Lexical Meaning Is Not Extension

Pietroski develops Chomsky's recurring examples of lexical flexibility and polysemy. `London`, `book`, `window`, `house`, and ordinary substance terms can be used to foreground different aspects of cities, artifacts, openings, buildings, or materials. No single ordinary object necessarily supplies the invariant extension required by a simple reference-first semantics.

Empty terms create another problem. If lexical meanings were extensions, `unicorn` and `centaur` would have the same meaning because both have empty actual extensions. Yet the expressions combine differently with other words and support different thoughts. Replacing actual extensions with possible-world extensions does not by itself explain the fine-grained conditions speakers associate with the terms.

Scientific identifications also should not be read directly into ordinary lexical meaning. The technical equation of water with H2O serves specialized explanatory purposes. Ordinary uses of `water` can remain sensitive to source, function, mixture, and human interests. Pietroski treats this flexibility as evidence about human concepts, not as noise to be eliminated before semantics begins.

## Conditions on Reference Rather Than Referents

The chapter's positive proposal is that meanings impose conditions on possible acts of use and reference without themselves being extensions. Lexical meanings provide structured, combinable resources. Syntax determines how those resources are assembled, and the resulting expressions constrain what speakers can intelligibly do with them. Context, thought, perception, and intention then help determine particular acts of reference.

This position preserves systematic semantic facts while denying that meanings determine extensions. It also leaves room for global or construction-wide properties of complex meanings. Composition need not be exhausted by assigning every lexical item a semantic value and computing a sentence extension through local function application.

Pietroski's formulation should be kept distinct from a general denial of compositionality. He argues that meanings compose in empirically constrained ways. His objection is to a particular extensional account of what the inputs and outputs of composition must be.

## What Remains Scientifically Tractable

The chapter identifies several semantic targets that remain systematic:

- the number and distribution of readings associated with an uttered string;
- correlations between syntactic structure and interpretation;
- constraints on anaphora, control, extraction, and scope;
- lexical conditions on use and possible reference;
- the ways lexical meanings interact when combined;
- the internal computational and conceptual resources that make these patterns possible.

The resulting research program is narrower than a theory assigning truth conditions to every sentence, but it is not empty. It asks how human language connects structured expressions to concepts and how those internal resources constrain interpretation and use.

## Relation to Language Models

The chapter does not discuss LLMs, and it does not establish that distributional representations possess Chomskyan meanings. Its relevance is diagnostic. It blocks several shortcuts in contemporary model interpretation.

First, failure to establish stable reference does not show that a model has no semantic organization. Second, evidence for structured lexical or compositional behavior does not by itself establish worldly reference. Third, an internal representation can constrain use without being an extension or truth condition. Finally, behavioral success with polysemy or ambiguity must be separated from a theory of the internal resources that produce it.

For LLM assessment, the appropriate questions include whether a model distinguishes structurally available readings, represents lexical conditions that generalize across contexts, composes them productively, and connects those resources to reference-fixing capacities. These are related tests, not one binary test for meaning.

## Limitations and Interpretive Cautions

- Pietroski offers a sympathetic reconstruction and extension of Chomsky's remarks, not an experimentally tested complete semantics.
- Examples of lexical flexibility challenge simple extension-first theories but do not by themselves rule out every externalist or truth-conditional account.
- The chapter does not supply a full processing theory of how speakers select interpretations in context.
- Internalism is compatible with speakers standing in worldly relations; it should not be restated as the claim that reference never occurs.
- The language-model implications are later interpretation, not conclusions drawn in the chapter.
