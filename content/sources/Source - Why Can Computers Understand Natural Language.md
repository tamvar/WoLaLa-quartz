## Summary
Juan Luis Gastaldi argues that the success of word embeddings and related neural NLP methods reflects an underlying structuralist image of language more than a straightforward reference-first or representation-first philosophy of meaning. The paper is important because it tries to reconstruct the conceptual assumptions behind word-embedding methods rather than treating them as purely technical devices. For WoLaLa, it is a useful bridge between distributional semantics, structuralist linguistics, and current arguments about what kinds of understanding computer language systems can plausibly support.

## Key Points
- The paper asks what conception of language is presupposed by word embeddings and related neural NLP methods.
- It argues that the usual slogan linking distributional methods to a generic use theory of meaning is too crude.
- Instead, it traces the stronger conceptual affinity to structuralist and distributionalist traditions running from Harris back toward Saussure and Hjelmslev.
- The paper presents word embeddings as inheriting matrix-model and term-context approaches rather than as inexplicable neural magic.
- For WoLaLa, it matters because it clarifies what kind of philosophical interpretation distributional success does and does not support.

## Details
Gastaldi's starting point is the common public question: if computers now seem to "understand" language, what picture of language made that possible? He argues that this question is often answered badly, either by mystifying neural methods or by attaching them too quickly to a generic philosophy-of-language picture centered on reference, truth, and representation.

The paper instead reconstructs a different lineage. Word embeddings can be understood through matrix models, vector-space models, and distributional semantic models in which linguistic units are represented by their positions in a structured relational system. On this reading, their conceptual background is closer to structuralist linguistics and Harrisian distributionalism than to a direct claim that words gain meaning because systems grasp worldly reference in a humanlike way.

This is useful for WoLaLa because it tightens the interpretation of distributional success. The paper does not deny that embeddings can support impressive language processing. It denies that their success should be redescribed too quickly as solving the classic philosophy-of-language problem of reference or understanding in the strongest sense. What they support more clearly is a structuralist picture in which linguistic value is heavily relational and system-internal.

## Interpretation
This source is a brief conceptual bridge rather than a final philosophical verdict. It helps clarify why many claims around embeddings and LLMs oscillate between overstatement and under-description. The right historical comparison is often not "machines now have reference" but "machines exploit a powerful structural-relational conception of language whose philosophical implications are still contested."

## Limits or Open Questions
The paper is interpretive and historically reconstructive rather than an empirical test of LLMs. Its value lies in conceptual clarification: it sharpens what kind of language theory embedding success naturally supports.
