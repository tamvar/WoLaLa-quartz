## Summary
Adam H. Marblestone, Greg Wayne, and Konrad P. Kording argue that deep learning and neuroscience should be treated as partially converging research programs rather than as unrelated enterprises. Their key proposal is that brains may also be usefully analyzed in terms of optimized cost functions, structured architectures, and learning rules, while machine learning can borrow hypotheses from neuroscience about internal training signals, specialized systems, and developmental organization. For WoLaLa, the paper is a concise bridge source linking [[../overviews/Neuroscientific Perspectives Overview|Neuroscientific Perspectives]] (strand 8) with [[../overviews/Mind Design and Reverse Engineering Overview|Mind Design and Reverse Engineering]] (strand 7).

## WoLaLa Relevance
This source is useful because it sharpens what a serious brain-model comparison would require. Similar performance or representational overlap is not enough on its own. Marblestone, Wayne, and Kording emphasize a wider explanatory frame:

- what objective or cost function a system is optimizing
- how learning signals are generated or propagated
- what architectural specialization organizes the system

That framework helps WoLaLa keep apart three different things that are often blurred:

- historical analogy between earlier neural modeling and current deep learning
- methodological bridge-building between AI and neuroscience
- stronger claims of explanatory equivalence between brains and language models

## Key Points
- The paper argues for renewed convergence between deep learning and neuroscience.
- It highlights cost functions, learning rules, and architectures as shared explanatory axes.
- It treats biological systems as likely to involve internally generated training signals and multiple specialized subsystems.
- Its value lies in methodological integration, not in claiming that current deep networks already explain the brain.

## Limitation Or Open Question
The paper is a hypothesis-and-theory article rather than a language-specific empirical study. Its strongest contribution is a research program, not a direct demonstration that any existing language model is neurally adequate. The main open question is which language-relevant model-brain comparisons in the repository can actually be strengthened by this framework rather than merely name-checking it.
