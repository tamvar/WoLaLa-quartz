## Summary
Gary Lupyan and Rick Dale argue that language structure is shaped partly by the social environments in which languages are learned and used. Using large-scale typological and demographic data, they propose that languages used in larger, more contact-heavy populations tend to simplify inflectional morphology and rely more on lexical strategies, while smaller-group languages preserve or develop greater morphological complexity. For WoLaLa, the paper matters because it links language structure to social and learning ecology rather than treating grammar as autonomous from use conditions.

## Key Points
- The paper argues that grammar is not independent of the social environments in which languages circulate.
- Large populations and adult-learning contact settings are associated with reduced inflectional complexity and greater reliance on lexical strategies.
- The authors propose a `linguistic niche hypothesis` on analogy with ecological adaptation.
- The argument connects morphology, acquisition difficulty, and cultural transmission.
- For WoLaLa, the paper broadens the cognitive strand into a genuinely social and cultural explanation of linguistic form.

## Limitation Or Open Question
The paper is typological and correlational. Its value here is not that it proves one causal story beyond doubt, but that it makes social-learning environment a serious explanatory variable for linguistic structure.
