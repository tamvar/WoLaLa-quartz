## Summary
Crain and Pietroski defend universal-grammar-style acquisition arguments against the idea that linguistic structure can be explained away as a simple product of environmental input. The article argues that the standard nature-versus-nurture framing is too crude and that language acquisition requires attention to the internally structured space of grammatical possibilities available to the learner. For WoLaLa, the paper functions as a concise statement of the nativist side of the acquisition dispute that later language-model work is often said to pressure.

## WoLaLa Relevance
This source is valuable less as a decisive verdict than as a clear articulation of one side of a still-live debate. It helps specify what defenders of universal grammar take the acquisition problem to be: not mere exposure to data, but the rapid emergence of structured grammatical knowledge from sparse and underdetermining evidence. That makes it an important counterweight whenever current model success is invoked as evidence that rich prior linguistic structure is unnecessary.

## Key Points
- The paper argues that the usual nature-versus-nurture opposition is too simple for language acquisition.
- Universal grammar is presented as a hypothesis about the constrained hypothesis space available to learners.
- The acquisition problem is framed around underdetermination and the character of the resulting knowledge, not only around surface performance.
- The source is best read as a position statement that clarifies what strong nativist arguments are claiming.

## Limitation Or Open Question
The article is a theoretical defense rather than a direct computational test. Its main usefulness is contrastive: it clarifies what later statistical and neural learnability work would need to answer. The open question is how much of this argument survives once explicit model learners begin to reproduce more structure from less data.
