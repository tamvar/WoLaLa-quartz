## Summary
Harvey Lederman and Kyle Mahowald ask whether LLMs are best understood as cultural technologies that only transmit derivative meaning, or as limited agents whose behavior is better explained by beliefs, desires, and intentions. Their central contribution is a new challenge to "bibliotechnism": cases of novel reference, where LLMs appear to coin new names for new entities.

## Key Points
- Source fact: the paper develops `bibliotechnism`, the view that LLMs are more like libraries or printing presses than like agents.
- Source fact: it argues that bibliotechnism can explain how LLMs generate novel but still derivatively meaningful text.
- Source fact: it introduces the `Novel Reference Problem`, where an LLM appears to generate genuinely new referring expressions rather than merely derivative reproductions of prior human reference.
- Source fact: the authors argue that novel reference is better explained if LLMs have a limited form of agency, including beliefs, desires, and intentions.
- Source fact: the paper connects this argument to interpretationism in the philosophy of mind, where intentional states are attributed when they best explain behavior.

## Details
Lederman and Mahowald begin from the idea, associated with Gopnik and others, that LLMs might be cultural technologies rather than agents. On this picture, they process and transmit human-written text without originating meaning in their own right.

The paper strengthens this position first. It argues that novelty in generated text is not by itself enough to refute bibliotechnism, because even new strings can inherit derivative meaning from meaningful human input. The authors use n-gram style examples and causal-chain reasoning to explain how copied or recombined text might still count as derivatively meaningful.

Their main challenge is more ambitious. Some outputs seem to involve novel reference: a model invents a new name and uses it to pick out a novel entity. These cases are harder to explain if all meaning and reference must piggyback on prior human tokens. The paper argues that once bibliotechnism is stretched far enough to explain such cases, the simpler explanation may instead be that LLMs have a limited kind of agency.

## Synthesis / Interpretation
Within the WoLaLa meaning/reference cluster, this paper sharpens the contrast between derivative meaning, reference, and agency. It pushes beyond [[Source - Do Language Models Refer|Mandelkern and Linzen]], who defend reference under an externalist account, by asking whether some LLM behavior is better explained by intentional attitudes rather than by speech-community inheritance alone.

It also pushes against a simple text-only skepticism. The paper does not merely ask whether LLMs can refer, but whether some forms of semantic novelty force a shift from library-like metaphors to librarian-like ones.
