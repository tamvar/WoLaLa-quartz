## Summary
Bernardo Goncalves argues that the long-running dispute over the Turing test is clarified if it is treated not as a badly specified laboratory benchmark but as a thought experiment. On this reading, Turing's imitation game was meant to reframe the concept of intelligence, probe objections, and motivate hypotheses about machine learning rather than supply a fully operational modern test protocol. For WoLaLa, the source matters because it helps separate historical interpretation of the Turing test from today's narrower benchmark talk.

## WoLaLa Relevance
This source primarily supports [[../overviews/Historical Perspectives on Language, Mind, and Modeling Overview|Historical Perspectives on Language, Mind, and Modeling]] and secondarily [[../overviews/Critical and Skeptical Perspectives Overview|Critical and Skeptical Perspectives]]. It is useful because it stops the historical Turing test from being flattened into either a failed benchmark or a decisive gold standard. In relation to [[../sources/Source - Computing Machinery and Intelligence|Turing's 1950 paper]], it argues that the imitation game was meant to do conceptual work: to expose what is and is not shown by linguistic imitation, and to illuminate how objections shift when intelligence is recast behaviorally.

## Limitation Or Open Question
The source is interpretive rather than foundational. Its value depends on whether the thought-experiment reconstruction captures Turing's intentions and the paper's best use today. The open question is how far that reconstruction should guide present evaluation practice for systems that differ sharply from the systems Turing imagined.
