## Summary

John Collins and Georges Rey identify a tension between Chomsky's scientific use of representational vocabulary and his skepticism about intentional notions in natural science. Early generative and acquisition theories describe speakers or children as representing grammars, testing hypotheses, misrepresenting inputs, and computing over content-bearing states. Later Chomskyan remarks often treat ordinary intentional attribution as interest-relative and unsuitable for scientific explanation.

The authors disagree about how to resolve the tension. Collins develops a nonintentional interpretation in which a theorist's formal representation classifies internal states without requiring the speaker to represent the grammar as such. Rey argues that this move becomes difficult once acquisition and interfaces are considered: language, perception, thought, and action need a `common coin` that explains sensitivity to the same abstract linguistic objects. The chapter ends with a live architectural problem, not a settled attribution of intentionalism to Chomsky.

## What Intentionality Adds

Intentionality is the aboutness or content-directedness of mental states and representations. Collins and Rey emphasize several characteristics. An intentional state can be about an object or condition; it presents that target under an aspect; it can be assessed as accurate, satisfied, or appropriate; and it need not imply that its apparent object exists. Thoughts about fictional or impossible objects still have content.

These features distinguish intentionality from mere covariance or physical sensitivity. They also make intentional explanation pervasive in cognitive science. Perception, memory, reasoning, language processing, and action are commonly explained by states that represent things as being a certain way.

The issue is not whether folk descriptions are scientifically infallible. The issue is whether mature cognitive theories require content-bearing internal states or can replace them with nonintentional formal and causal descriptions.

## The Apparent Intentionalist Chomsky

Generative linguistics has often used robustly representational language. Children are said to form hypotheses about grammars, internalize rules, and converge on a system that represents linguistic structure. A competent speaker can misanalyze or misrepresent an expression. Computational systems operate over internally differentiated states whose roles depend on the linguistic objects they concern.

Chomsky's example of a `cognizing missile` strengthens the appearance of intentionalism. A system can be described as having internal knowledge that guides discrimination and action even without consciousness or verbal report. On this reading, linguistic competence is a contentful state of an organism, and acquisition is rationally sensitive to evidence.

Such descriptions appear to require more than a theorist assigning a convenient formal model. The learner's transitions seem responsive to the content of inputs and hypotheses, and the mature system appears able to distinguish correct from incorrect analyses.

## Chomsky's Censure of Intentional Explanation

Other Chomskyan passages express deep skepticism. Ordinary attributions of belief, knowledge, representation, or reference vary with interests and circumstances. Chomsky doubts that these notions identify stable kinds for natural science and sometimes treats mentalistic vocabulary as informal description rather than literal theoretical commitment.

This creates the chapter's central interpretive problem. If `representation` is used only technically, what does the technical notion contribute? If it retains aboutness, aspect, and correctness conditions, it looks intentional. If those properties are removed, it may become no more than a label for a physical or functional state.

The authors resist resolving the issue by stipulation. Calling a state a representation does not explain whether the organism, the theorist, or only an external interpreter supplies its content.

## A Nonintentional Formal Reading

Collins develops a Goodman-inspired alternative. A grammar or formal model can be an abstract theoretical posit that tracks and classifies states of the speaker. The theorist represents the speaker's system; the speaker need not internally represent the grammar as a theory. An `x-representation` can be defined by its place in a formal or causal organization without carrying ordinary intentional content.

This approach can describe mature competence economically. The grammar captures counterfactual regularities in how the system responds to inputs and generates expressions. Principles-and-parameters acquisition can likewise be modeled as triggering changes in an internal system rather than as conscious or unconscious inference over hypotheses.

The proposal sets aside two common intentionalist arguments. A scientific theory can represent generalizations without the organism itself representing those generalizations, and a system can transition among states without explicitly inferring the rule that justifies the transition.

The price is explanatory. Triggering and formal classification may redescribe sensitivity to linguistic structure without explaining how the organism is sensitive to precisely those abstract properties.

## The Common-Coin Problem

Rey presses the difficulty through interfaces. I-language must interact with perception, sensorimotor systems, thought, and action. These systems need to coordinate around words, phrases, interpretations, and other linguistic objects. The relevant properties are often abstract and nonlocal; they are not simply physical features available to a transducer.

A transducer can convert local energy patterns into internal signals. It does not by itself explain how several systems are jointly sensitive to a phrase, dependency, or intended content. Intentional representations offer a common coin because different states can be about the same object under related aspects and can be evaluated against it.

The same problem arises in acquisition. A parameter-setting device that changes state when exposed to input may predict convergence, but calling the input a `trigger` does not explain why the change is rationally related to the grammar manifested by the environment. Hypothesis testing over contentful representations offers one explanation. A nonintentional theory must offer an alternative with comparable explanatory reach.

## An Unresolved Architectural Disagreement

Collins and Rey do not converge on a final verdict. A nonintentional reading may be viable for a formally isolated account of syntax, especially when the grammar is treated as the scientist's model of system states. It becomes less comfortable when acquisition, cross-system access, misrepresentation, and coordinated action enter the picture.

The intentionalist reading makes these relations intelligible but sits uneasily with Chomsky's explicit skepticism about interest-relative intentional notions. The chapter therefore identifies pressure on both sides:

- nonintentionalism owes an account of structural sensitivity and interface coordination;
- intentionalism owes a scientifically disciplined account of content that does not merely import everyday attribution;
- interpretation of Chomsky must preserve his actual oscillation rather than smoothing it into one stable doctrine.

## Relation to Language Models

The chapter does not analyze LLMs, but its distinctions are directly relevant to model interpretation. At least four claims are often conflated:

- observers can interpret a model's output as being about something;
- internal states causally covary with task-relevant features;
- a mechanistic theory identifies states that represent those features for the model;
- the model has intentional states with aboutness, aspect, and correctness conditions.

Behavioral content ascription establishes the first claim most readily. Probes and interventions may support the second and, with a strong functional account, parts of the third. They do not automatically establish the fourth.

The common-coin problem supplies a constructive test. If representations coordinate language, perception, memory, planning, and action around the same objects under stable aspects, a content-bearing explanation may gain force. Yet a successful multimodal interface could still be described nonintentionally as a causal architecture. The decisive question is which description explains generalization, error, and cross-system coordination better.

## Limitations and Interpretive Cautions

- The authors explicitly disagree; the chapter should not be summarized as a unified verdict.
- The intentionalist and nonintentionalist interpretations concern scientific architecture, not whether speakers casually use representational vocabulary.
- `Representation` can label different theoretical roles; its occurrence alone does not prove intentional content.
- The common-coin argument identifies explanatory pressure but does not prove that no nonintentional alternative can work.
- The language-model application is a later extension and should not be attributed to Collins, Rey, or Chomsky.

## Open Questions

- Can a nonintentional architecture explain abstract linguistic sensitivity across acquisition, perception, thought, and action without merely redescribing it?
- What evidence would show that an internal model state represents a feature for the system rather than only for an external theorist?
- Does multimodal or agentic coordination strengthen intentional interpretation, or only expand the causal role of nonintentional representations?
