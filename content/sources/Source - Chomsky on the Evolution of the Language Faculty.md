## Summary

Anne Reboul reconstructs Chomsky’s longstanding position that language is a species-specific biological endowment, did not evolve primarily as an adaptation for communication, and emerged discontinuously. Minimalism makes this package more coherent by reducing the narrow faculty of language to Merge or recursion. Reboul then extends the proposal: the distinctively human capacity may also include a conceptual-intentional system whose concepts are decoupled from immediate perception and action, supporting language as a tool for thought.

## What Evolved?

The biological target is not an E-language such as English or Tagalog, which changes historically and can be acquired by any human child. It is the faculty that allows an individual to develop an I-language. Earlier generative theories attributed rich, language-specific structure to that faculty, making gradual adaptation seem plausible. Minimalism narrows the target to a simple computational operation that yields discrete infinity and interfaces with conceptual-intentional and sensory-motor systems.

On this view, Merge may be an exaptation rather than an adaptation for communication. If recursive computation existed in another domain and became available to language in a single change, a saltationist account becomes possible. Because an operation is not naturally acquired “in part,” the reduction of FLN to Merge supports discontinuity more readily than a many-component language faculty would.

## Communication, Thought, and Decoupling

Reboul examines the claim that language is primarily for thought. Animal communication systems are limited in signal inventory and typically tied to present environments or action. Human linguistic contents are unbounded and strongly decoupled: people discuss absent, abstract, impossible, or fictional entities without an immediate behavioral affordance.

She argues that Merge alone does not explain where this range of contents comes from. Human concepts appear less tightly bound to perception and action than animal concepts. The crucial innovation may therefore be the interface between Merge and a distinctively decoupled conceptual-intentional system. This motivates extending FLN beyond Merge.

The proposal shifts comparative research toward two questions: whether nonhuman animals possess degrees of decoupled representation, and how combinatorial syntax interacts with conceptual systems. It also opens contact with internalist semantics and pragmatics, where interpretation is not exhausted by reference to external objects.

## WoLaLa Interpretation

The chapter distinguishes producing communicative signals from having a language-like system for structured thought. Language models are optimized on communicative artifacts and can generate unbounded-seeming text, but these facts alone do not establish a human-like conceptual-intentional interface or evolutionary continuity.

Its decoupling criterion can nevertheless guide evaluation. Models can be tested on counterfactual, fictional, abstract, and action-independent reasoning while controlling for memorized templates. Success would bear on computational capacity; it would not settle whether the model’s representations have the biological or experiential role attributed to human concepts.

## Limitations

- The saltationist and exaptationist account remains programmatic and underdetermined by direct evolutionary evidence.
- Comparative claims about animal recursion and concepts depend on contested interpretations of behavior.
- Extending FLN to include the conceptual-intentional system is Reboul’s proposal, not simply Chomsky’s established position.
- The chapter offers no empirical analysis of language models.
