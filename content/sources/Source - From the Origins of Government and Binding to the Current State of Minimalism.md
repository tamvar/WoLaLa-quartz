## Summary

Artemis Alexiadou and Terje Lohndal reconstruct the development of generative linguistics from transformational grammar through Principles and Parameters, Government and Binding (GB), and the Minimalist Program. Across these changes, the durable object of study is I-language: a psychologically real system that generates structured form–meaning pairings. The major shift is explanatory. GB distributed grammatical work across a rich set of modules and constraints; Minimalism asks how much of that machinery can be derived from a smaller computational core, interface conditions, and more general principles of efficient computation.

## Historical Argument

The authors organize generative inquiry around three questions: what constitutes knowledge of language, how it is acquired, and how it is used. Early transformational grammar combined phrase-structure rules with transformations that mapped underlying structures to surface forms. Principles and Parameters reframed acquisition as selection within a constrained space: invariant principles specify common organization, while parameter settings capture systematic variation.

GB implemented this program through interacting subtheories, including X-bar theory, Case, Binding, Theta theory, Control, Bounding, and Government. This architecture increased empirical coverage but also made Universal Grammar theoretically rich. Minimalism responds by treating that richness as something to explain rather than stipulate. Merge replaces separate construction and transformation systems; interfaces with sound and meaning constrain legible outputs; and third-factor principles such as computational efficiency are asked to carry work previously assigned to language-specific machinery.

## Current Tendencies

The chapter does not present Minimalism as a finished theory. It surveys continuing disputes about feature inventories and their organization, labeling and phrase structure, lexicalist versus decompositional architectures, and the correct location of crosslinguistic variation. Work on multilingual grammars is especially important because mixed systems can reveal whether features, roots, and functional structure are distributed as a theory predicts.

The change from GB to Minimalism also changes the acquisition–evolution tradeoff. A rich UG can constrain acquisition strongly but is harder to explain evolutionarily. A minimal UG seeks an account in which the language-specific innovation is small and much observable complexity follows from interfaces, general computation, and experience. The authors regard this reduction as improving contact with broader cognitive research, while leaving substantive empirical questions open.

## WoLaLa Interpretation

The chapter supplies an architecture map for comparing linguistic theories with language models. It warns against treating any recovered dependency, feature, or hierarchy as evidence that a model implements the corresponding GB or Minimalist mechanism. Similar outputs may arise from different computations. Relevant comparisons must distinguish the formal object generated, the procedure that builds it, the memory available to that procedure, the learning problem, and the interfaces that use the result.

It also clarifies that `generative linguistics` names an explanatory tradition about human linguistic knowledge; it is not synonymous with `generative AI`. Neural language models may become evidence in this debate only through tests that discriminate among architectures and learning hypotheses.

## Limitations

- The chapter is a selective internal history, not a neutral comparison with functional, constructionist, or usage-based traditions.
- It documents programmatic simplification more clearly than it establishes that all GB-era generalizations have received Minimalist explanations.
- Its discussion of language models is indirect; it offers distinctions for analysis, not empirical findings about transformers.
