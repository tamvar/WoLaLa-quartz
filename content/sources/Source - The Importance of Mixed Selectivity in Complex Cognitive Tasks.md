## Summary
Mattia Rigotti, Omri Barak, Melissa R. Warden, Xiao-Jing Wang, Nathaniel D. Daw, Earl K. Miller, and Stefano Fusi argue that complex cognition benefits from neural populations whose responses mix multiple task-relevant variables rather than cleanly encoding one factor at a time. Their claim is that such mixed selectivity greatly expands the dimensionality of neural representations, making flexible context-sensitive behavior easier to support. The paper is important for WoLaLa as a neuroscience and representation bridge, not as direct evidence about language models. It gives a compact reason to be cautious about overly neat one-feature or one-function pictures of cognition: flexible performance may depend on distributed, heterogeneous coding that only becomes intelligible at the population level.

## WoLaLa Relevance
This source primarily supports [[../overviews/Neuroscientific Perspectives Overview|Neuroscientific Perspectives]] and secondarily [[../overviews/Mind Design and Reverse Engineering Overview|Mind Design and Reverse Engineering]]. It helps the wiki articulate why representational complexity matters when people compare biological and artificial systems. In WoLaLa terms, it is a bridge source for questions about whether apparently language-relevant behavior should be explained through simple local features, richer distributed representations, or broader system organization. It also supplies a useful caution for mechanistic interpretation: explanatory progress may require understanding how multiple variables are jointly encoded rather than expecting clean one-unit-to-one-concept mappings. That caution belongs to comparative background, not to direct evidence about language-model internals.

## Limitation Or Open Question
This is not a language-model paper, and it should not be treated as direct evidence about LLM internals. Its value is comparative and methodological. The open question is which lessons about mixed, high-dimensional coding can responsibly transfer from cognitive neuroscience to language-model interpretation without collapsing biological and artificial cases into the same explanatory framework.
