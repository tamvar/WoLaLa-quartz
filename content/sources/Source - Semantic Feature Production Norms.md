## Summary
Ken McRae, George S. Cree, Mark S. Seidenberg, and Chris McNorgan's "Semantic feature production norms for a large set of living and nonliving things" provides a large human-generated dataset of semantic features for 541 concrete concepts. Participants listed properties such as perceptual attributes, functions, locations, and taxonomic relations, yielding a resource that has been widely used to study conceptual structure, category-specific deficits, semantic similarity, and feature-based representation. For WoLaLa, the paper matters because it gives a disciplined human baseline for what rich concept knowledge looks like when it is represented as articulated feature structure rather than only as distributional co-occurrence.

## WoLaLa Relevance
This source supports WoLaLa as a resource and framing paper rather than as a direct LLM study. It is useful because many later model-comparison arguments rely on claims about human semantic knowledge, conceptual features, and category structure. McRae et al. make those structures explicit in a form that can be compared with embeddings, probes, feature-recovery methods, neuropsychological evidence, and model-brain alignment work.

## Key Points
- The paper collects semantic feature norms for 541 living and nonliving concepts from roughly 725 participants.
- Participants produce feature descriptions such as perceptual properties, functions, locations, actions, and taxonomic information.
- The resulting norms support analyses of feature distinctiveness, shared structure, and correlations among properties across concepts.
- The paper is important because it turns informal talk about `concept knowledge` into a reusable empirical resource with explicit representational structure.
- For WoLaLa, it helps distinguish distributional success from stronger claims about human-like conceptual organization.
- The source also matters for neuroscience-adjacent work because feature norms have been used to study category-specific semantic deficits and the organization of conceptual knowledge in the brain.

## Details
The paper's main contribution is infrastructural but intellectually important. Rather than proposing a new language model or benchmark, it constructs a large semantic-norm dataset that makes human concept knowledge inspectable at the feature level. A concept such as `camel` or `hammer` is represented through structured properties generated by participants, allowing later researchers to analyze which features are distinctive, which are shared, and how feature correlations shape semantic similarity and category behavior.

That matters for WoLaLa because many current arguments about model meaning, world knowledge, and representation depend on an implicit comparison class. If a model is said to capture semantic structure, one reasonable question is: compared with what human account of semantic structure? McRae et al. provide one concrete answer. Their norms represent concept knowledge as articulated bundles of features rather than only as lexical neighborhoods or downstream task behavior.

The source is also a bridge between cognitive and neuroscientific strands. The paper situates the norms in relation to research on semantic memory and category-specific deficits, especially the contrast between living and nonliving things. That makes it relevant when later work uses feature-based semantic structure to interpret brain data, lesion patterns, or representational-similarity results. In WoLaLa terms, the paper helps keep model-to-human comparison tied to explicit cognitive structure rather than only to aggregate performance scores.

## Interpretation
This is best treated as a human baseline resource and conceptual anchor, not as direct evidence about LLM competence. It becomes valuable when paired with sources such as [[../sources/Source - Semantic Projection Recovers Rich Human Knowledge from Word Embeddings|Semantic Projection Recovers Rich Human Knowledge from Word Embeddings]], [[../sources/Source - Word Meaning in Minds and Machines|Word Meaning in Minds and Machines]], or neuroscience-facing comparison work. Those later papers ask whether model representations recover feature-like knowledge or align with human semantic organization. McRae et al. clarify what that target structure looks like in a classic feature-based form.

At the same time, the paper should not be inflated into a complete theory of meaning. Feature norms are especially useful for concrete concepts and human-generated descriptive knowledge, but they do not settle questions about reference, compositionality, discourse meaning, or grounded action. Their value lies in providing one disciplined human semantic baseline among several, not in replacing broader semantic theory.

## Limitation Or Open Question
The norms are richest for concrete object concepts and participant-accessible descriptive features. They are therefore a partial view of semantic knowledge rather than a general account of meaning. An open question for WoLaLa is how far feature-structured human concept data can serve as a comparison target for modern language models, especially for abstract concepts or contextual meaning.
