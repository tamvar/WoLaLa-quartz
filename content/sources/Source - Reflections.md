## Summary

Noam Chomsky reflects on the transition from American structuralism’s taxonomic and procedural program to generative linguistics as a search for explanatory theories of I-language and Universal Grammar. He argues that competence must be distinguished from performance, that a language is a stored internal computational system rather than a finite set of habits, and that current Minimalist work seeks explanations satisfying both learnability and evolvability through Merge and general principles of efficient computation.

## From Procedures to Theories

Chomsky contrasts generative inquiry with the mid-century American structuralist consensus. Zellig Harris described procedures an analyst could apply to linguistic data and resisted ranking alternative analyses as competing theories. Charles Hockett treated language as changing habits and analogies, doubting that a well-defined grammar could be encoded in a physically variable brain.

Chomsky replies that science routinely idealizes away from noise to characterize underlying systems. A finite organism can know an unbounded procedure just as a person can understand that there is no largest integer. Turing architecture makes the distinction explicit: a stored program remains the same as memory constraints are relaxed. The grammar’s generative capacity should therefore not be confused with the finite utterances a person has produced or can process at one time.

The generative project treats language as an internal, individual, intensional system. A grammar is the linguist’s theory of that I-language. A deeper theory of UG addresses the species-typical initial state. Development reflects interaction among innate structure, external data, and third factors such as computational efficiency and other properties of cognition and the brain.

## Learnability and Evolvability

Early generative theories expanded descriptive resources because structuralist procedures could not capture the entities needed for explanation. This exposed poverty-of-stimulus problems but made UG appear complex. A rich initial state helps learnability while making recent biological evolution harder to explain.

The Minimalist Program seeks a smaller common solution. Binary set formation, or Merge, yields unbounded hierarchical expressions. Internal Merge derives displacement and reconstruction; structure dependence follows because operations target hierarchical objects rather than linear order. Externalization into speech, sign, or touch is secondary and imposes ordering requirements not intrinsic to narrow syntax.

Chomsky presents these as steps toward genuine explanation, not a completed theory. Hidden assumptions remain even in analyses of simple sentences, and removing them may change current conceptions substantially.

## Communication and Meaning

The chapter argues that language’s basic design is not optimized for communication. Its core generates structured expressions of thought; sensorimotor externalization can make them communicable but also introduces complexity. Chomsky also proposes treating much `formal semantics` as syntax-like symbolic manipulation, reserving semantics more narrowly for reference and denotation, which he doubts characterize language itself in a straightforward way.

## WoLaLa Interpretation

The essay clarifies why fluent text generation does not by itself answer what a system knows. Corpus behavior, internal program, resource-bounded inference, and output policy are different explanatory levels. For language models, the analogous decomposition includes learned parameters, architecture, context-bound computation, and decoding.

Its critique of habit and analogy also applies to underspecified appeals to scale or pattern learning. An adequate account must say which representations and operations yield the generalization. At the same time, Chomsky’s Minimalist claims remain hypotheses about human language; structural behavior in a model is not evidence of literal Merge without discriminating analysis.

## Limitations

- This is a programmatic retrospective by the central participant, not an external history of linguistics.
- Competing structuralist, functional, usage-based, and statistical traditions receive limited and critical treatment.
- The claimed derivations from Merge depend on additional assumptions that remain under active debate.
- Remarks about modern statistical work are not a systematic assessment of contemporary LLMs.
