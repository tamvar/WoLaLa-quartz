## Summary

Lisa Lai-Shen Cheng and James Griffiths identify empirical generalizations that outlast particular versions of generative syntax. Their central cases concern hierarchical structure, nonlocal dependencies, and unpronounced positions. The chapter’s main methodological claim is that Chomsky’s program—explicit grammars, systematic acceptability judgments, and a search for crosslinguistic generalizations—created a productive research method even when the theoretical analysis of individual facts changed.

## Enduring Generalizations

Nonlocal dependencies relate expressions that are separated in the pronounced string, as in wh-questions, relative clauses, topicalization, and other displacement constructions. The decisive observation is that the licensing and interpretation of these relations depend on hierarchical configurations, not simply linear distance. Constituency, c-command, locality domains, and the internal structure of phrases therefore have empirical consequences.

The authors emphasize that natural language contains several kinds of nonlocal relation rather than a single undifferentiated movement phenomenon. Dependencies differ in whether the displaced item is pronounced at the top or tail, whether the relation crosses islands or finite boundaries, and whether it is restricted by binding domains. This typology remains useful because new constructions can be tested against established diagnostics and apparently distinct classes can be investigated for a deeper common analysis.

Gaps are equally heterogeneous. Some are linked to an overt displaced expression; others behave more like silent pronouns, elided material, or positions licensed by a construction. The ubiquity of meaningful but unpronounced structure motivates representations richer than surface word strings, although the chapter does not claim that every gap has the same ontology.

## Evidence and Method

Generative syntax uses acceptability judgments to obtain both positive and negative evidence, while acknowledging that acceptability is affected by processing, discourse, and cultural factors. The authors cite experimental work showing high convergence between informal and formal judgment collection for many large effects. That defense is bounded: judgments require controlled contrasts and do not make performance factors disappear.

The chapter also stresses research accumulation. A robust generalization becomes a diagnostic for investigating other phenomena, supporting typologies and reductive explanations. “Enduring” therefore means empirically productive and crosslinguistically resilient, not theoretically immutable.

## WoLaLa Interpretation

The chapter offers a demanding target for language-model evaluation. Success on local agreement or word-order prediction is not enough; tests should examine structurally matched contrasts, long-distance dependencies, island sensitivity, silent structure, and generalization across constructions and languages. Behavioral success would show sensitivity to the relevant generalization, but not by itself establish that a model uses movement, traces, c-command, or any particular generative formalism.

Its methodology also supports separating three questions: whether a pattern is present in model behavior, whether an internal representation tracks the relevant structural variable, and whether the model’s learning mechanism explains the human generalization. Those are distinct evidential burdens.

## Limitations

- The inventory is deliberately selective and centered on discoveries the authors regard as characteristically generative.
- Theoretical controversy remains over the analysis and ontology of many dependencies and gaps.
- Judgment reliability does not settle whether judgments alone provide an adequate theory of grammar.
- The chapter predates current LLM evaluation and makes no direct claims about neural models.
