## Summary
Noam Chomsky's review of B. F. Skinner's *Verbal Behavior* is one of the classic turning points in twentieth-century thinking about language and mind. Chomsky argues that Skinner's behaviorist vocabulary of stimulus, response, reinforcement, and conditioning fails to explain linguistic productivity, structure, meaning, or acquisition. He treats Skinner's framework not as an incomplete but promising science of language, but as a terminological recoding of ordinary mentalistic descriptions that leaves the real explanatory problems untouched. For WoLaLa, the source matters because it is a compact and historically consequential statement of why surface behavioral regularity is not enough, by itself, to explain language.

## Key Points
- Chomsky argues that Skinner's core explanatory terms become empty when stretched from laboratory conditioning to ordinary language use.
- The review insists that linguistic creativity, structural organization, and open-ended generalization cannot be handled by simple reinforcement-based behaviorist models.
- Chomsky treats language acquisition as requiring more than feedback-driven shaping, emphasizing innate structure, hypothesis formation, and abstract generalization.
- The article is historically important because it helped discredit behaviorism as the dominant framework for explaining higher cognition and language.
- For WoLaLa, it remains relevant as an early warning against confusing successful behavioral description with adequate explanatory theory.

## Details
The review advances on several fronts at once. First, Chomsky attacks Skinner's use of terms such as `stimulus`, `response`, `reinforcement`, and `probability`, arguing that they have clear experimental meanings only in tightly controlled settings. Once Skinner applies them to real linguistic behavior, Chomsky argues, they become vague cover terms for intention, interest, belief, expectation, and other familiar notions rather than explanatory replacements for them.

Second, Chomsky argues that the framework cannot account for the generative character of language. Speakers understand and produce indefinitely many novel utterances, distinguish well-formed from malformed strings they have never encountered before, and generalize in ways that are not captured by simple similarity or reward history. The review therefore presses a point that remained central in later linguistic theory: a language theory must explain structured productivity, not merely redescribe observed habits.

Third, the review expands from linguistic performance to acquisition and cognition. Chomsky points to latent learning, curiosity-driven exploration, imitation, and biologically constrained learning as reasons to doubt that reinforcement is the necessary engine of language acquisition. The effect is not merely anti-Skinner polemic. It is a broader argument that language and mind require an explanatory framework sensitive to internal structure, representational organization, and species-specific capacities.

## Interpretation
This source should not be treated as if it already contained the whole mature generative program, and it should not be read as a direct argument about contemporary neural language models. Its historical value is more precise. It shows why twentieth-century linguistics came to resist behaviorist reduction and why performance alone was not accepted as a sufficient explanatory standard.

That matters now because some current LLM debates replay a structurally similar dispute. Modern language models can display strong behavioral competence without transparent, theory-mirroring internal descriptions. Chomsky's review does not by itself show that such systems fail. But it does explain why many linguists continue to ask whether observed performance is accompanied by the right kind of structural explanation, rather than taking output quality alone as decisive.
