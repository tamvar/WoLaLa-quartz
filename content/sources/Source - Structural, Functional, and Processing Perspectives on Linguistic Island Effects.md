## Summary
Liu, Winckel, Abeille, Hemforth, and Gibson review the long-running literature on linguistic island effects and argue that most of the phenomenon is better explained by discourse, frequency, and memory than by strong innate syntactic constraints. They retain a narrower role for structural explanation, especially around coordination islands, but push against taking island effects as a clean vindication of syntax-first nativist theory. For WoLaLa, the article is an excellent bridge because it keeps a classic theoretical-linguistics topic connected to processing, usage, and cognitive explanation.

## Key Points
- The paper reviews island effects through structural, functional, and processing lenses rather than assuming a single explanatory level.
- It argues that most island phenomena are better explained by discourse, frequency, and memory than by purely syntactic constraints.
- Coordination islands are treated as the strongest remaining case for a specifically structural constraint.
- The article shows how a classical generative topic can be re-evaluated through experimental and processing evidence.
- It matters for WoLaLa because it models a disciplined way to compare formal and usage/processing explanations rather than treating them as slogans.

## Details
Island effects have long been a central exhibit in arguments that human syntax contains strong innate constraints. Liu and colleagues revisit that terrain with a deliberately pluralist review. Instead of asking only whether a sentence is generated by syntax, they ask which parts of the evidence are better explained by structural constraints and which are better explained by discourse conditions, processing difficulty, memory demands, frequency, and interference.

Their review centers on filler-gap constructions and the famous cases where extraction appears unacceptable. The striking claim is not that syntax never matters. It is that most of the empirical landscape looks less like a unitary syntactic prohibition and more like a family of effects with heterogeneous explanations. On their account, coordination islands remain the best candidate for a genuinely structural constraint, while many other island phenomena are more plausibly tied to usage and processing.

For WoLaLa, the source is useful in two ways. First, it gives strand 4 a serious example of a longstanding theoretical topic being reinterpreted through broader cognitive and experimental evidence. Second, it helps resist a simplistic contrast between formal linguistics and statistical or processing approaches. The paper does not merely replace syntax with usage. It redistributes explanatory weight across levels and asks what the evidence actually supports.

## Interpretation
This article is a strong theory-facing bridge because it shows what it looks like when a classical theoretical-literature dispute is re-opened with experimental, discourse, and processing considerations in view. It therefore belongs near [[../sources/Source - A Probabilistic Model of Syntactic and Semantic Acquisition|A Probabilistic Model of Syntactic and Semantic Acquisition]], which also bears on how much structure one should posit and where explanatory burden should fall.

It does not settle the broader argument between generative and usage-based approaches, but it makes that argument more evidence-sensitive and less binary.
