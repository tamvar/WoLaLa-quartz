## Summary

Nicholas Allott and Neil Smith distinguish two often-conflated concepts of modularity. Fodorian modules are processing systems, especially input systems, characterized by properties such as speed, automaticity, domain specificity, and informational encapsulation. Chomskyan modules are analytically separable competence systems, such as I-language, governed by proprietary principles. The accounts are compatible because a language faculty can supply representations to parsers and production systems without itself being a Fodorian input module.

## Two Modular Programs

Fodor’s modularity thesis concerns how information is processed. Perceptual systems and perhaps language parsing operate rapidly and obligatorily, use specialized representations, and are partly insulated from central beliefs. Fodor’s claim that central cognition is comparatively nonmodular does not deny the existence of a language competence system.

Chomsky’s modularity concerns explanatory decomposition. I-language can be studied in abstraction from parsing, production, memory, and pragmatics because it is characterized by distinctive principles. GB once divided grammar itself into many submodules; Minimalism seeks to derive those generalizations without preserving every GB component. The broader faculty view remains: cognition contains partially independent systems whose interaction produces behavior.

The competence–performance relation needs causal care. A grammar is not a temporal parsing algorithm, but linguists still explain interpretations and acceptability partly by what the grammar generates. The authors favor a view in which the I-language is a system accessed by input and output processes. This preserves its distinction from performance while allowing it to constrain processing.

## Evidence from Dissociation

Dissociations provide the strongest evidence for separable systems, although single dissociations can mislead and double dissociations are more probative. Aphasic patterns can separate phonological and syntactic abilities. Prosopagnosia and Capgras syndrome separate face recognition from associated affective response.

The polyglot savant Christopher supplies a central language case. His linguistic capacities are strong relative to general intelligence, yet he and neurotypical controls failed to learn structure-independent counting rules when presented as language. The controls could solve comparable explicit puzzles. The authors infer that a language-learning system restricts the hypotheses entertained even when general cognition could represent the rule.

## Alternatives

Connectionist and deep-learning accounts are criticized as both too weak and too strong: they may miss humanly available structure while learning patterns people do not treat as possible language. Developmental `modularization` accepts eventual specialization but denies that it is innate. Massive modularity extends task-specific systems throughout central cognition. The chapter distinguishes these positions rather than treating all uses of `module` as equivalent.

## WoLaLa Interpretation

The two-module distinction is valuable for model explanation. A specialized processing pathway, an analytically isolable competence, and an emergent cluster of representations are different claims. Ablations, probes, and behavioral dissociations can suggest separability, but they do not automatically establish informational encapsulation or innate domain specificity.

Language models also make the competence–performance problem concrete. Their learned parameters, inference procedure, context window, and decoding policy contribute differently to output. Reverse engineering should identify which component supports a behavior before naming a module.

## Limitations

- Many classical modularity properties are matters of degree rather than a single decisive checklist.
- Neuropsychological dissociations require alternative deficit and access explanations to be excluded.
- The discussion is sympathetic to Chomskyan and Fodorian decomposition and treats connectionist alternatives briefly.
- Claims about contemporary deep learning are programmatic rather than experimentally developed.
