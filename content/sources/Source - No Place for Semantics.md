## Summary
J. C. Nyíri's 1971 article "No Place for Semantics" denies that semantics has an autonomous theoretical place within linguistic theory. Beginning from a conception of meaning as a word's role in the totality of language, Nyíri distinguishes relations internal to language from extralinguistic acts such as pointing and acting. His central claim is asymmetrical: every semantic difference that can be expressed by purely linguistic means must in principle be representable syntactically, while relations to nonlinguistic action cannot be captured by adding an independent semantic component. He develops this argument through changes in Chomskyan subcategorization and a criticism of Katz and Fodor's semantic markers and projection rules. Semantic description can remain heuristically useful, but it does not constitute a separate level of linguistic theory.

## Key Points

- Meaning is understood as role or place within the interconnected totality of language, not as a detachable inventory added after syntax.
- Nyíri distinguishes intralinguistic constraints from extralinguistic moves such as pointing and acting. The latter connect language with the world but are not another internal component of linguistic description.
- Linguistically expressible semantic differences must, in principle, be syntactically representable. The syntax-semantics distinction therefore loses its status as a natural division between autonomous theoretical domains.
- The expansion of Chomskyan subcategorization illustrates how distinctions treated as semantic can be incorporated into increasingly articulated formal structure.
- Katz and Fodor's semantic markers and projection rules do not, on Nyíri's account, establish an independent theoretical function for semantics; they redescribe distinctions that syntax must ultimately represent.
- Semantic description remains useful as a heuristic for discovering underlying formal distinctions, without becoming an autonomous explanatory component.

## Nyíri's Argument

Nyíri begins from a role-based conception of meaning: the meaning of an expression is its place within the total organization of language. This organization includes systematic relations among expressions, but language also participates in acts that extend beyond language itself. Pointing, practical action, and other uses can connect an utterance to an extralinguistic situation. Nyíri does not treat those connections as evidence for a second, internally organized semantic level parallel to syntax. They mark a boundary between what linguistic structure can represent and what speakers do with language in the world.

Within the intralinguistic domain, Nyíri argues that a difference in meaning that is expressible through language must be reflected in formal structure. His discussion of Chomskyan subcategorization supplies the principal linguistic route to this claim. As subcategorization becomes more finely articulated, distinctions that had appeared to require separate semantic information can be represented through restrictions on distribution and structural combination. The development is evidence for enlarging and refining formal description, not for installing an autonomous semantic theory alongside it.

The criticism of Katz and Fodor follows from this representability thesis. Semantic markers and projection rules appear to introduce a separate system for determining readings, but Nyíri argues that any linguistically operative distinctions they capture must already be representable in the formal organization of language. Semantic analysis may guide the investigator toward distinctions that a syntactic description has not yet made explicit. That is a heuristic role in inquiry, not evidence that semantics is an independent theoretical component.

## Interpretation

Nyíri's position should not be presented as a compromise between reducing meaning to form and preserving an autonomous semantics. He substantially rejects an independent theoretical semantics, while retaining two qualifications: semantic description can assist the discovery of formal structure, and extralinguistic action or use cannot simply be redescribed as another intralinguistic rule system.

This distinction is relevant to later debates about language models only by interpretation. It sharpens questions about whether apparent semantic differences are recoverable from linguistic structure and distribution, and about what remains dependent on action, context, or worldly use. The article does not argue that statistical language models possess meaning, that structural success is sufficient for understanding, or that present neural systems vindicate Nyíri's program.

## Limitation Or Open Question

The paper predates modern computational linguistics and develops its case within early debates over generative linguistics and semantic theory. Its representability thesis is a strong theoretical claim, not an empirical result about contemporary models. The continuing question is whether formal or distributional representations can exhaust the linguistically expressible distinctions at issue, and how extralinguistic use should enter an adequate theory of meaning.
