## Summary
Richard M. Rorty's edited volume *The Linguistic Turn: Essays in Philosophical Method* is a historically important source container for the idea that philosophical problems are often best approached through language, logic, and linguistic method rather than through direct metaphysical system-building. This note treats the volume selectively rather than as a unified doctrine. For WoLaLa, its importance is historical and methodological: it maps the internal divisions of mid-century linguistic philosophy, distinguishes ideal-language from ordinary-language approaches, and preserves a direct bridge from philosophy of language to linguistic theory through pieces such as Quine's "Semantic Ascent" and Jerrold Katz's "The Philosophical Relevance of Linguistic Theory."

## Selective Scope
This source page treats the anthology selectively as a historical bridge for:

- the split between ideal-language and ordinary-language approaches
- Quinean semantic ascent and related metalinguistic strategies

## Key Points
- The anthology is valuable as a map of positions and disputes, not as a single thesis.
- Its contents explicitly separate ideal-language and ordinary-language strands within linguistic philosophy rather than collapsing them into one movement.
- The preface frames the collection as a record of how philosophers used linguistic method to defend, criticize, or redirect philosophical inquiry.
- The volume is especially relevant to WoLaLa because it includes sources and discussions that connect philosophy of language to questions about meaning, semantics, and linguistic theory.
- Rorty's later retrospective essays make the book useful not only as a 1960s anthology but as a historical self-assessment of what the linguistic turn did and did not accomplish.

## Details
The local file shows that the volume is an edited collection first published in 1967 and reissued in 1992 with two retrospective essays by Rorty. The contents make its structure unusually explicit. Part I gathers classic statements of the thesis that philosophical questions are questions of language, including Schlick, Carnap, Bergmann, Ryle, and related ideal-language material. Part II turns to metaphilosophical problems within ideal-language philosophy, including Copi, Bergmann, Black, and Quine's "Semantic Ascent." Part III shifts to ordinary-language philosophy through Chisholm, Passmore, Hare, Geach, Urmson, Cavell, and the Austin-centered discussions. Part IV then recapitulates and criticizes the tradition, closing with Katz on the philosophical relevance of linguistic theory and Bar-Hillel on rational philosophical discussion, before Rorty's retrospective essays revisit the anthology's significance.

That structure matters for WoLaLa because current disputes about language models often inherit fragments of these traditions without naming them. Some arguments treat philosophical clarification of LLM meaning claims as a problem of language about language: what counts as `understanding`, `reference`, or `semantics`, and how should those terms be used? Others inherit ordinary-language caution about how philosophical theory outruns actual use. Still others take linguistic theory itself to be philosophically consequential, especially when debates about generative grammar, formal semantics, or distributional meaning become proxies for deeper claims about mind and explanation.

The volume therefore helps in two ways. Historically, it shows that language-centered philosophical method was always internally divided, with no single consensus about whether analysis should proceed through logical reconstruction, use, ordinary practice, or formal linguistic theory. Methodologically, it keeps WoLaLa from flattening older philosophy-of-language background into one generic prehistory. The anthology is best treated as a structured map of arguments whose afterlives still shape present debates about semantics, reference, linguistic adequacy, and the evidential force of language behavior.

## Interpretation
This source should be treated as a selective deep source container. It should not be summarized as though Rorty or the anthology endorsed one uniform linguistic program. Its WoLaLa value comes from staging several different ways philosophy became language-centered, and from showing that debates about meaning, theory, and method were already branching into forms that remain relevant for contemporary language-model interpretation.
