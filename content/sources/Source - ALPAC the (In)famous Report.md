## Summary
John Hutchins's "ALPAC: the (in)famous report" is a retrospective historical note on the 1966 Automatic Language Processing Advisory Committee report and its consequences for machine translation. Its main contribution is not a new theory of language or intelligence, but a precise reminder that evaluation, public narrative, and funding judgments can reshape an entire field for decades. For WoLaLa, the piece is valuable because it links language-technology history to present concerns about benchmark interpretation, overreaction to apparent failure, and the institutional consequences of how progress is measured.

## WoLaLa Relevance
This source primarily supports [[../overviews/Historical Perspectives on Language, Mind, and Modeling Overview|Historical Perspectives on Language, Mind, and Modeling]] and secondarily [[../overviews/Applications and Best Practices Overview|Applications and Best Practices]]. It shows that disputes about evaluation are not only technical. Hutchins reconstructs how the ALPAC report became shorthand for machine translation failure, how its wording is often misremembered, and how assessment standards and funding decisions can redirect language technology for a generation. That historical caution is relevant whenever current language-model results are treated as either decisive breakthroughs or decisive dead ends.

## Limitation Or Open Question
This is a short retrospective note, not the ALPAC report itself and not a full history of machine translation. Its value is interpretive and historical. The open question is which present evaluation practices in LLM research risk replaying the same mismatch between local assessment criteria, public narrative, and long-run field development.
