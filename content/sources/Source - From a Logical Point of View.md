## Summary
W. V. O. Quine's *From a Logical Point of View* is a historically central essay collection on ontology, analyticity, meaning, reference, and logical method. This note treats the volume selectively rather than as a single thesis. For WoLaLa, the collection matters because it supplies several of the background disputes that still underwrite arguments about semantics, linguistic competence, and whether meaning can be reconstructed from formal or inferential structure alone.

## Selective Scope
This source page treats the collection selectively as historical and philosophical background for:

- the critique of analyticity in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism"
- ontological commitment in "On What There Is"
- meaning and linguistics in "The Problem of Meaning in Linguistics"
- reference and modality in the later essays on reference

## Key Points
- The collection ties questions of meaning to wider disputes about analyticity, ontology, and logical method.
- "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" destabilizes a clean analytic-synthetic boundary and pressures attempts to ground meaning in purely definitional structure.
- "The Problem of Meaning in Linguistics" connects philosophical worries about meaning to linguistically relevant notions of synonymy and semantic criterion.
- The reference essays matter because they make semantic commitment answerable to broader theories of ontology and logical form.
- For WoLaLa, the collection is useful less as a direct statement about language models than as a background source on what semantic explanation is being asked to do.

## Details
The local volume makes its selective relevance unusually clear from the table of contents and preface. Quine identifies two recurring themes: the problem of meaning, especially around analyticity, and the problem of ontological commitment, especially around universals and what our theories say there is. Those are not peripheral issues for WoLaLa. They are exactly the kinds of background assumptions that surface when arguments about LLM semantics ask whether meaning depends on reference, inferential role, usage, formal organization, or world-involving commitment.

"On What There Is" matters because it ties ontology to the commitments of our best theories rather than to ordinary naming alone. Even when current LLM debates do not cite Quine directly, similar pressures appear whenever semantic claims are asked to answer not just what words do, but what kinds of entities or structures our explanations presuppose.

"Two Dogmas of Empiricism" matters because it attacks a sharp distinction between truths that are analytic by meaning alone and truths that depend on fact. That critique complicates any simple picture in which linguistic meaning could be captured by a clean stock of definitions or self-contained internal relations. For WoLaLa, this is useful background when sources dispute whether semantic competence is exhausted by internal textual structure or requires broader inferential and worldly integration.

"The Problem of Meaning in Linguistics" is the most directly relevant essay for this repository. It connects philosophical worries about meaning and synonymy to the aspirations of linguistics. The essay helps explain why later disputes over semantic theory, distributional evidence, and formal representation are not only technical questions but also inherit a deeper uncertainty about what counts as a satisfactory criterion of meaning in the first place.

## Interpretation
This collection should be treated as selective deep historical background rather than as a source to flatten into one master doctrine. Quine is not here because he offers a ready-made answer to LLM meaning. He matters because many present options in WoLaLa's meaning strand are easier to see against his pressures on analyticity, synonymy, ontological commitment, and reference.

That is also why the note belongs secondarily in the historical strand. The collection clarifies the mid-century philosophical setting in which later debates about semantics, cognition, and formal systems took shape.
