## Summary

Lila Gleitman gives an autobiographical account of how Chomsky’s critique of behaviorism helped make language a central problem for cognitive science. Her research asks how children acquire structured linguistic knowledge and word meanings from sparse, ambiguous, and uneven experience. Evidence from input deprivation and syntactic bootstrapping supports a constrained learner: experience is indispensable, but the learner’s prior organization determines which evidence can be used and which generalizations are possible.

## From Distribution to Cognitive Structure

Gleitman contrasts two formative influences. Zellig Harris pursued distributional procedures that moved from observable linguistic patterns toward categories and relations. Chomsky argued that surface distributions are artifacts of an underlying mental system and that passive association cannot explain structured generalization. Gleitman’s later work preserves part of both insights: the child uses distributional evidence, but only through a mind equipped to represent relevant linguistic structure.

Input studies undermine simple “motherese” explanations. Caregiver speech varies across communities, and special simplification does not determine whether children acquire language. More extreme cases are more revealing. Blind children learn visual vocabulary despite radically different perceptual evidence; deaf children can acquire language normally when they receive accessible signed input; Nicaraguan Sign Language shows a community developing increasingly systematic structure. These findings constrain claims about which surface experiences are necessary without showing that input is dispensable.

## Syntactic Bootstrapping

The central positive mechanism is syntactic bootstrapping. Verbs occur in argument structures that correlate with aspects of meaning. A learner can use the number and arrangement of noun phrases, complement types, and alternations to narrow a verb’s interpretation. Syntax does not uniquely determine meaning, but it provides structured evidence that complements observation of events.

Gleitman describes lexical learning as “propose, but verify.” Children may form candidate meanings in relatively discrete episodes and test them across subsequent uses. This differs from both direct ostension and unconstrained accumulation of associations. The learner must integrate conceptual possibilities, syntactic frames, and situational evidence.

## Machines and Understanding

The chapter briefly contrasts classical AI’s logic-centered account with probability-driven learning. Probabilistic models improve coverage but can exploit regularities that fail on improbable yet intelligible cases. Gleitman uses ambiguous examples and humorous headlines to argue that humans combine expectations with flexible world knowledge: they notice the statistically ordinary reading but can adopt an unlikely interpretation when reality demands it.

This discussion predates current model capabilities and should not be treated as a contemporary benchmark verdict. Its durable point is methodological: high-probability continuation and understanding of a structurally possible, contextually unusual event are different targets.

## WoLaLa Interpretation

The chapter complicates a simple generative-versus-distributional opposition. Distribution is evidence, but its explanatory role depends on the representational hypotheses available to the learner. Language-model studies should therefore ask not only how much data a model receives, but which structural distinctions its architecture and objective make learnable.

Comparisons with children also require scale and access controls. Models receive vastly more text and different feedback, while children combine linguistic, perceptual, social, and conceptual information. Similar behavior does not imply similar acquisition.

## Limitations

- The chapter is an edited autobiographical interview, not a systematic review.
- Several sections of the earlier source article were omitted, so the evidential narrative is selective.
- The machine-learning discussion reflects a pre-current-LLM moment and cannot support claims about present systems.
- Deprivation cases require ethical and confound-sensitive interpretation.
