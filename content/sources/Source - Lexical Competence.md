## Summary
Diego Marconi asks what distinguishes a system that genuinely understands a natural language from one that merely processes it. The book’s main contribution to the present WoLaLa cluster is a two-aspect picture of lexical competence: understanding a word involves both inferential competence, or the ability to manage semantically relevant connections among words, and referential competence, or the ability to apply words to the world. Marconi uses that distinction to resist both a purely truth-conditional picture of word meaning and any simple collapse of meaning into reference, encyclopedic knowledge, or holistic world knowledge.

## Key Points
- the book treats lexical competence as central to the broader question of what makes language understanding possible in humans and artificial systems.
- it argues that meaning postulates and inferential relations capture only one part of lexical competence, not the whole content of word understanding.
- it develops a distinction between inferential competence and referential competence, and treats them as partly independent abilities.
- it argues against identifying lexical meaning either with unrestricted encyclopedic knowledge or with a strong objectivist notion of reference.
- it connects semantic normativity to patterns of correction and relative competence within a linguistic community rather than to a fully private grasp of meaning.
- it closes by asking what kinds of performances would justify attributing referential competence to artificial systems.

## Details
Marconi frames the book around a question that remains directly relevant to LLM debates: what separates a system that merely manipulates linguistic material from one that can be said to understand language? He approaches that problem through word understanding rather than sentence-level truth conditions alone, arguing that lexical meaning is where several familiar semantic programs become inadequate.

The first major move is negative. Inferential role, meaning postulates, and dictionary-style specifications matter, but they do not exhaust lexical competence. Marconi’s discussion of the dictionary/encyclopedia boundary is meant to show that not every true belief involving a word belongs equally to its meaning, and that one still needs a principled account of which information counts as constitutive of competence.

The second major move is the book’s positive proposal. Marconi distinguishes inferential competence from referential competence. Inferential competence concerns the network of abilities involved in semantic inference, paraphrase, definition, synonymy, and related word-to-word relations. Referential competence concerns the ability to apply words to things in the world. The two abilities often cooperate, but they need not rise and fall together.

That distinction matters for the philosophy of meaning. Marconi argues that a speaker may be quite good at applying a word while knowing relatively little about its referent’s deeper nature, and conversely may possess rich descriptive information while showing weaker practical referential abilities. This lets him resist a simple appeal to Putnam’s division of linguistic labor as the whole story about competence, while still acknowledging that linguistic communities sustain norms of correction and deference.

The later chapters extend the point to objective reference, semantic normativity, and artificial systems. Marconi argues that successful communication depends first on convergence in application rather than on a heavyweight objectivist theory of reference. He also treats more-competent and less-competent uses as central to semantic normativity. In the final chapter, he asks what kinds of performances would justify attributing referential competence to artificial systems, making the book an unexpectedly direct bridge from pre-LLM lexical semantics to current debates about whether language-processing systems genuinely understand the words they use.

## Interpretation
This source deepens the active meaning/reference cluster by adding a more articulated competence vocabulary. It supports a picture in which meaning, inference, and reference are connected but not identical, and in which linguistic understanding cannot be reduced either to string-level inferential links or to a single externalist test of reference.

It sits especially well between [[Source - Distributional Models of Word Meaning|Lenci]] and [[Source - Do Language Models Refer|Mandelkern and Linzen]]. Like Lenci, Marconi treats lexical meaning as something richer than mere denotation and more structured than raw world knowledge. Like Mandelkern and Linzen, he refuses to equate semantic success with a maximally strong conception of internal grasp. But his two-aspect framework also sharpens a question that matters for LLM evaluation: when a system appears semantically competent, is the evidence mainly inferential, mainly referential, or some unstable combination of the two?
