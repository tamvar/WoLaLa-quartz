## Summary
Matthew Mandelkern and Tal Linzen ask whether language-model outputs are genuinely referential or merely a simulacrum of meaningful language use. Drawing on externalist philosophy of language, they argue that the usual grounding objection is too quick: even text-only inputs may carry the right natural histories to put LM words into referential contact with the world.

## Key Points
- Source fact: the paper frames the core question as whether LM outputs are meaningful in the relevant sense, focusing specifically on whether their words refer.
- Source fact: it rejects a simple grounding objection that says reference cannot arise because LMs learn only from strings.
- Source fact: the authors argue that LM inputs are not bare strings but strings with natural histories that may connect them to referents.
- Source fact: the paper draws on externalism to argue that reference does not require the rich beliefs, experiences, or discriminatory capacities that LMs most plausibly lack.
- Source fact: it leaves open whether LMs count as members of a speech community in the right way, and identifies that question as crucial.

## Details
The paper contrasts meaningless but interpretable patterns, such as ant trails that accidentally resemble a sentence, with ordinary meaningful linguistic use. It then asks whether LM outputs are more like the accidental pattern or like a false but meaningful human utterance.

Mandelkern and Linzen's central move is externalist. On their presentation, reference depends less on a speaker's internal grasp of a referent and more on standing in the right historical relation to a linguistic community and its word-world links. That weakens a natural anti-LM argument: the absence of direct perception or rich domain competence does not by itself show that LM words fail to refer.

At the same time, the paper is not a blanket endorsement of strong semantic competence claims. It narrows the issue to referentiality, and treats speech-community membership as an open and difficult question.

## Synthesis / Interpretation
This source is a central anchor for the reference side of the debate. It does not settle whether LMs understand language, but it directly challenges the move from "trained on form" to "cannot possibly refer."

It should therefore be read alongside [[Source - Climbing Towards NLU|Bender and Koller]], not as a simple rebuttal but as a narrower claim: a model may fail to learn meaning in the strong communicative-intent sense while still supporting a defensible account of reference.
