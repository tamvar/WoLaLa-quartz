## Summary
Tal Linzen and Marco Baroni's "Syntactic Structure from Deep Learning" is a survey of work testing whether deep neural networks acquire human-like syntactic knowledge from raw language data. The paper argues that deep learning results make classic linguistic questions newly empirical, but not newly simple. Contemporary networks can show surprising competence on targeted syntactic phenomena such as agreement and filler-gap dependencies, yet that does not settle whether they learn syntax in the same way humans do or whether their internal representations match linguistically meaningful structure. For WoLaLa, the paper is a central bridge between model performance results and theoretical-linguistic interpretation.

## Key Points
- The review separates practical deep-learning success from the narrower question of theoretically significant syntactic competence.
- It organizes the literature around agreement, filler-gap dependencies, other syntactic phenomena, and the question of what internal representations encode.
- The paper argues that networks can learn more syntax than strong anti-statistical positions would predict, but still fall short of human competence in important ways.
- It treats probing and targeted evaluation as evidential tools rather than as automatic demonstrations of human-like grammatical knowledge.
- For WoLaLa, it is valuable because it frames the empirical syntactic literature as bearing on long-standing linguistic debates rather than as a self-contained benchmark field.

## Details
The local file is an author manuscript version dated 2020, while the article is identified by its Annual Review DOI and later publication record. The contents page shows the paper's compact but strategic organization: an overview of deep learning for language processing, a review of long-distance agreement, a section on other syntactic phenomena, a section on what the network's internal representations encode, and a discussion section on implications for linguistic theory.

That structure lets the paper do two things at once. It surveys a now substantial body of model-testing work, and it repeatedly asks what sort of theoretical conclusion those results actually warrant. Linzen and Baroni emphasize that success on engineered applications does not automatically resolve the older acquisition and competence debates that motivated linguistic theorists to care about syntax in the first place. At the same time, they also show that targeted empirical results have made strong dismissals of data-driven learning harder to maintain. The resulting position is neither triumphalist nor dismissive: deep networks matter for syntax, but the meaning of that evidence depends on the standard of explanation being used.

## Interpretation
This source should be treated as a central deep bridge note. It is not just another evaluation paper, because its point is not mainly to report one benchmark result. Its point is to interpret a whole research program and to ask what deep-learning evidence should count for in theoretical linguistics. That makes it especially important for keeping WoLaLa's theory strand connected to actual empirical work without collapsing theory into benchmark scorekeeping.
