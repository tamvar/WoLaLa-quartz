## Summary
Chen et al. ask whether natural-language explanations generated by language models actually let users predict how those models would behave on related counterfactual cases. Their answer is mostly no: plausible explanations need not be precise explanations. For WoLaLa, this is a strong strand-6 caution against treating explanation fluency as transparency.

## WoLaLa Relevance
This source primarily supports [[../overviews/Applications and Best Practices Overview|Applications and Best Practices]] and secondarily [[../overviews/Critical and Skeptical Perspectives Overview|Critical and Skeptical Perspectives]]. It is especially useful because it introduces counterfactual simulatability as a stronger standard for explanation quality. Instead of asking whether an explanation sounds reasonable, the paper asks whether it helps a person form an accurate mental model of the system's behavior on nearby cases. That makes it a good methodological warning for scholarly use, interpretability claims, and evaluation design.

## Limitation Or Open Question
The paper studies a demanding standard and specific tasks, so it should not be read as proving that all natural-language explanations are worthless. The open question is which explanation settings can be made genuinely behavior-revealing rather than merely plausible.
