## Summary
Emma Strubell, Ananya Ganesh, and Andrew McCallum quantify the financial and environmental cost of training and developing large NLP models, then argue that these costs are methodological and policy issues rather than mere engineering side effects. Their core point is not only that large models consume energy, but that compute-intensive research practices shape who can participate, what can be reproduced, and which claims become cheap enough to test. For WoLaLa, the paper matters because it makes resource inequality part of language-model methodology.

## WoLaLa Relevance
This source strengthens strand 6 by showing that evaluation and research design are constrained by material conditions. If training, retuning, or reproducing a result requires unusually expensive hardware and repeated large-scale search, then methodological best practice cannot be separated from questions of access, reporting, and research equity. That matters for WoLaLa because language-centered evaluation is often discussed as though only benchmarks and prompt design matter.

It also matters for strand 5 because it reframes model development as a socially structured research practice. The paper helps connect multilingual and SSH concerns about unequal language coverage to upstream inequalities in compute access and experimental reproducibility.

## Key Points
- The paper estimates training and development costs for several influential NLP model families.
- It argues that hyperparameter search and repeated retraining can dominate the total cost of a result.
- Financial and environmental costs also function as equity barriers that advantage well-resourced labs.
- The authors recommend reporting training cost, runtime sensitivity, and access conditions more explicitly.

## Limitation Or Open Question
The specific cost estimates are historically local to 2019 hardware, energy mixes, and model sizes. The enduring value of the paper is therefore not the exact numbers but the methodological argument that resource intensity affects what counts as responsible and reproducible research.
