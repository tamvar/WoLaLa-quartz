## Summary
Hollander and Olney investigate whether symbolic and embodied processing tradeoffs found for nouns and adjectives also hold for verbs. Across three experiments, they argue that verb meaning reflects both distributional linguistic structure and embodied perceptual information, with context and task demands affecting which signal dominates. For WoLaLa, the paper is a useful narrow bridge because it tests the symbolic-versus-embodied relation on verbs, a domain especially relevant to language processing rather than only object concepts.

## WoLaLa Relevance
This source primarily supports [[../overviews/Cultural, Cognitive, and SSH Perspectives Overview|Cultural, Cognitive, and SSH Perspectives]] and secondarily [[../overviews/Neuroscientific Perspectives Overview|Neuroscientific Perspectives]]. The paper matters because it does not frame symbolic and embodied processing as a winner-take-all opposition. Instead it shows, using verbs, that distributional regularities and perceptual information can each explain part of semantic processing, with task structure determining their relative force. That makes it a good compact bridge between distributional language evidence and embodied-cognition pressure.

## Limitation Or Open Question
The study is tightly focused on verb-processing experiments, not on language models directly. The open question is whether its integrated symbolic-embodied pattern can inform how LLM semantic behavior should be interpreted, or whether it remains mainly a human psycholinguistic result.
