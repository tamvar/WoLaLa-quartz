## Summary

Diane Lillo-Martin surveys the indirect but substantial influence of generative linguistics on sign-language research. Chomsky wrote little about particular sign languages, yet the distinction between an abstract language system and its sensory-motor externalization predicts deep cross-modal commonalities alongside modality-specific effects. Evidence from syntax, acquisition, homesign, and emerging sign languages supports the status of signed languages as full natural languages while also exposing limits of overly strong modularity and idealized acquisition accounts.

## Syntax Across Modalities

Early sign-language linguistics first had to establish that signed systems were structured languages rather than gesture collections or encodings of speech. Generative analyses of American Sign Language identified constituent structure, subordination, agreement, null arguments, movement constraints, and the role of c-command. Nonmanual markings can spread over structurally defined domains, providing visible evidence of hierarchy.

The relevant prediction is not that sign and speech have identical surfaces. Hierarchical organization and operations such as Merge or Agree may be shared, while linearization, simultaneity, spatial morphology, and articulation reflect different sensory-motor interfaces. Sign languages therefore test whether proposed universals are genuinely linguistic or accidental properties of speech.

## Acquisition, Deprivation, and Emergence

Children exposed to an accessible natural sign language from birth follow developmental trajectories broadly parallel to spoken-language acquisition. The scientifically and ethically important contrast is delayed or degraded access. A deaf child may receive spoken input that is physically present but not linguistically accessible. Late first-language exposure produces lasting deficits and supplies clearer evidence for sensitive-period effects than ordinary second-language learning.

Children can also regularize inconsistent input. The case of “Simon,” whose late-learning parents produced variable ASL morphology, shows a child converging on more systematic patterns than the input providers. Homesign systems arise when deaf children lack conventional signed input, and Nicaraguan Sign Language demonstrates rapid communal emergence and increasing grammatical organization across cohorts. These cases show that input is necessary while raising questions about the learner’s contribution to structure.

## Cognitive Consequences

Language deprivation can affect theory of mind, executive function, numeracy, and other cognitive domains. Such findings complicate a view on which language and cognition develop independently. They may indicate that language supplies representational resources used by otherwise distinct systems, or that a stronger modular separation is mistaken. The chapter leaves this architectural issue open.

## WoLaLa Interpretation

The chapter makes modality a required dimension of claims about language-capable models. Systems trained primarily on text cannot establish that a proposed principle is independent of linear written form. Multimodal and signed-language models should be tested for shared hierarchical generalization as well as modality-specific organization.

It also warns against equating input availability with accessible evidence. Human comparisons must account for sensory access, age of first exposure, community, and language deprivation; model comparisons need analogous clarity about representation, preprocessing, and training signal.

## Limitations

- The survey covers representative findings rather than the full diversity of signed languages.
- Homesign and emerging-language evidence does not show that a complete grammar develops without social transmission.
- Delayed access is entangled with educational and social conditions and must be interpreted cautiously.
- The chapter has no direct analysis of multimodal or signed-language AI systems.
