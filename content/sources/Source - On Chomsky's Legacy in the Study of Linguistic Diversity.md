## Summary

Mark Baker argues that Chomsky’s personal concentration on a narrow set of languages nevertheless enabled a flourishing generative study of linguistic diversity. He characterizes three phases provocatively as neglecting diversity in *Aspects*, limiting it through principles and parameters in *Lectures on Government and Binding*, and apparently denying it in *The Minimalist Program*. Beneath those shifts, Baker finds a stable research ethos: formulate explicit grammars, seek interactions among phenomena, constrain variation by learnability, and explain surface differences through a shared architecture plus structured points of variation.

## Three Phases

The early focus on English was a tactical narrowing. Detailed analysis showed that superficially similar sentences could have different structures and that properties of a language form an interdependent system. Acquisition supplies a boundary condition: because children can acquire any human language, complex properties must either reflect shared endowment or follow from simpler learnable features interacting with general principles.

The GB/P&P phase made diversity central. Universal principles constrained possible grammars, while parameters modeled discrete, clustered differences. Comparative work expanded through close analyses of Romance, Germanic, Semitic, Asian, Indigenous, and other languages. Baker’s polysynthesis example illustrates the attraction of parameters: one abstract difference can have wide-ranging consequences for agreement, argument expression, word order, and question formation.

Minimalism proposes that there may be one computational system, with much crosslinguistic variation located in lexical features and externalization. Baker does not read this as a rejection of comparative syntax. He distinguishes two complementary routes to explanation: expanding the facts brought under analysis and reducing the assumptions needed to derive them. Minimalists emphasize the second; fieldworkers and comparative syntacticians continue the first.

## Parameters as an Open Program

Baker acknowledges unresolved questions about the size and format of parameters. Macroparameters seek broad typological clusters; microparameters capture fine-grained differences; hierarchical approaches let local settings create larger tendencies. The absence of consensus does not erase the empirical discoveries made while competing formulations were tested.

The chapter is explicitly partisan. It contrasts the Chomskyan view of languages as structured systems with accounts Baker portrays as collections of unrelated constructions. That contrast is useful as a statement of explanatory ambition, but it should not substitute for direct assessment of constructionist and functionalist theories.

## WoLaLa Interpretation

For language-model research, the chapter makes crosslinguistic variation an explanatory stress test. Aggregate multilingual performance cannot show whether a model has learned shared principles, language-specific lexical properties, families of constructions, or dataset correlations. Strong evidence would test predicted clusters of phenomena, low-resource generalization, transfer, and exceptions across typologically diverse languages.

It also cautions against treating English-centered success as evidence about language in general. A theory or model earns broader claims through systematic comparative coverage, not through the size of an English corpus.

## Limitations

- The three-phase narrative is interpretive and intentionally provocative.
- Baker advocates a generative comparative program and does not neutrally adjudicate alternatives.
- Parametric theory remains unsettled at macro, micro, and implementation levels.
- The chapter contains no direct evaluation of language models.
