## Summary

Stephen Crain and Rosalind Thornton present experimental evidence that four- and five-year-old children apply Binding Theory’s Principle C across five superficially different constructions. They argue that children unify declaratives, clefts, quantified phrases, crossover questions, and discourse sequences through hierarchical structure and Logical Form. Because children receive little or no negative evidence about the prohibited interpretations, the authors treat the generalization as evidence for innate linguistic constraints rather than piecemeal learning from surface distributions.

## Principle C and Logical Form

Principle C blocks coreference when a pronoun c-commands a referring expression such as a name or definite description. The restriction depends on hierarchical position, not word order. It can also apply to an unpronounced copy at Logical Form. In a cleft such as *It was Marcel who he said was the winner*, the pronounced name precedes the pronoun, but its lower copy lies in the pronoun’s c-command domain, blocking the coreferential interpretation.

The chapter treats this abstract representation as a source of unity. Structures that look different on the surface receive analyses that place the relevant expressions in the same structural relation. A theory based only on recurring adjacent strings would therefore miss the generalization the authors aim to explain.

## Experimental Evidence

The primary method is the Truth Value Judgment Task. Children watch a story and judge a puppet’s sentence in a context designed to make the sentence true only on the interpretation prohibited by Principle C. Rejection is evidence that the child cannot access that tempting interpretation, not merely that the sentence sounds unusual.

Across the reviewed experiments, children consistently reject Principle C violations in declarative sentences, clefts, sentences involving quantified noun phrases and null pronouns, crossover questions, and paired discourse sequences. Control items establish that children can accept the relevant lexical content and permissible reference patterns. The authors emphasize that the unattested meanings supply especially important evidence: children cannot learn from ordinary correction that an otherwise grammatical sentence lacks a particular interpretation.

## Argument and Disagreement

The inference to UG depends on more than early success. It combines early structure-sensitive knowledge, convergence across surface-diverse constructions, and the absence of direct negative evidence. Usage-based or statistical accounts would need to show how the input and domain-general mechanisms determine the same exclusions. The chapter argues that learning each construction separately is implausible; it does not experimentally compare every possible alternative learner.

## WoLaLa Interpretation

Principle C offers a high-value language-model benchmark because it concerns unavailable interpretations, not only preferred continuations. A model test should vary c-command while controlling lexical content, linear order, plausibility, and discourse. It should also distinguish probability shifts from categorical representational exclusions.

Model success would demonstrate a learned structural generalization, but would not establish human-like innateness. Unlike children, pretrained models receive vast text corpora, and their training signal can contain indirect distributional evidence. The acquisition claim and the representational test must therefore remain separate.

## Limitations

- The chapter argues from a focused family of experiments rather than a comprehensive comparison of acquisition theories.
- Experimental rejection can be influenced by task pragmatics despite the use of controls.
- The poverty-of-stimulus inference depends on a defensible account of the child’s available evidence.
- Human acquisition results do not directly determine what language models learn.
