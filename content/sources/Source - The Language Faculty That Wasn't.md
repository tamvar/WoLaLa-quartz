## Summary
Morten H. Christiansen and Nick Chater argue that even the reduced generative idea of a special language faculty centered on recursion is too strong. They propose instead that recursive language use emerges from domain-general sequence learning, cultural evolution, and processing constraints rather than from a language-specific recursive grammar module. For WoLaLa, the paper matters because it gives a compact usage-based reply to strong nativist claims while keeping the discussion tied to cognition, evolution, and learnability.

## WoLaLa Relevance
This source is a useful bridge between theoretical-linguistic disputes over recursion and broader cognitive questions about sequence learning, cultural evolution, and the relation between processing limits and grammatical structure.

## Key Points
- The paper argues that a special language faculty is difficult to reconcile with evolutionary considerations.
- It treats recursion as a case study in how apparently language-specific structure may emerge from domain-general sequence learning and cultural shaping.
- The authors connect recursion to learning and processing constraints rather than to a dedicated grammatical mechanism.
- The paper is an important usage-based counterpoint to minimal language-faculty claims.
- For WoLaLa, it helps place LLM-related learnability debates inside a longer dispute about what kind of cognitive machinery language actually requires.

## Limitation Or Open Question
The paper is a review-style theoretical intervention rather than a direct model comparison. Its main value here is to sharpen the kind of cognitive and evolutionary alternative that strong language-faculty claims face.
