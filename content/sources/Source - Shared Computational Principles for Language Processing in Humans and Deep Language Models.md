## Summary
Ariel Goldstein and many coauthors argue that human language processing and autoregressive deep language models share three broad computational principles: continuous next-word prediction before word onset, post-onset surprise or prediction-error updating, and contextual embedding-like representations of words in context. The source matters for WoLaLa because it is one of the clearest current attempts to treat large predictive language models as neuroscience-relevant computational comparators rather than merely engineering systems. It belongs primarily to [[../overviews/Neuroscientific Perspectives Overview|Neuroscientific Perspectives]] (strand 8) and secondarily to [[../overviews/Cultural, Cognitive, and SSH Perspectives Overview|Cultural, Cognitive, and SSH Perspectives]] (strand 5).

## WoLaLa Relevance
This source gives strand 8 an explicit flagship claim, not just a loose analogy. It argues that autoregressive language models are relevant to human language neuroscience because both systems engage in prediction, surprise updating, and context-sensitive representation while processing the same narrative input. That is a stronger and more organized comparison than saying only that `brains and models both process language`.

For WoLaLa, the paper is especially useful because it clarifies both the promise and the risk of this literature. The promise is that predictive language models may provide computational hypotheses for some aspects of neural language processing. The risk is that a shared high-level principle can be mistaken for shared internal mechanism. The source therefore works best as a bridge paper whose strongest claims still need careful qualification.

## Limitation Or Open Question
The central caution is that `shared computational principles` is broader than `shared implementation` or `shared explanation`. Even if brains and autoregressive models both predict upcoming words and encode contextual information, that does not show they realize those functions through the same architecture or that the model is a good theory of human language cognition in a broader sense.
