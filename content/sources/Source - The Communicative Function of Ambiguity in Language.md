## Summary
Steven T. Piantadosi, Harry Tily, and Edward Gibson argue that ambiguity is not merely a defect of language but a predictable outcome of efficient communication when context is informative about meaning. They combine an information-theoretic argument with empirical tests from English, German, and Dutch to claim that ambiguity can be a functional design feature. For WoLaLa, the paper matters because it directly challenges the idea that ambiguity counts against communicative or usage-based explanations of linguistic structure.

## Key Points
- The paper argues that efficient communicative systems should be ambiguous when context supplies enough disambiguating information.
- Ambiguity can reduce processing and storage costs by allowing the re-use of efficient forms across multiple meanings.
- The authors test the resulting predictions across lexical and grammatical ambiguity in three languages.
- The source is theory-facing because it pushes against views that treat pervasive ambiguity as evidence that language is not shaped by communication.
- For WoLaLa, the paper links information-theoretic explanation to broader debates about function, form, and the adequacy of usage-based accounts.

## Details
The paper starts from an apparent puzzle. If language were optimally designed for communication, one might expect one form per meaning and minimal ambiguity. Piantadosi, Tily, and Gibson argue that this expectation is wrong because it ignores contextual information. If context already narrows the space of plausible meanings, then forcing each linguistic form to encode every distinction directly is inefficient. Ambiguity becomes compatible with, and in some settings predicted by, a communicatively optimized code.

This matters for WoLaLa because ambiguity is one of the recurring pressure points between formalist and functionalist pictures of language. The paper offers a compact argument that some surface irregularities or many-to-one mappings need not count against communication-centered explanation. That makes it relevant not only to historical linguistics and information-theoretic accounts, but also to contemporary debates about whether language-model success supports usage-based or distributional traditions.

The article is also useful as a methodological anchor because it combines abstract argument with crosslinguistic empirical tests. It does not simply assert that communication matters; it derives predictions from a coding perspective and checks them against observable ambiguity patterns. In that respect it complements later bridge sources such as [[../sources/Source - Information Theory as a Bridge Between Language Function and Language Form|Information Theory as a Bridge Between Language Function and Language Form]].

## Interpretation
This source should be read as a functionalist pressure argument, not as a total theory of grammar. It shows that ambiguity can be positively compatible with efficient communication, but it does not imply that all grammatical structure is best explained by communicative optimization or that stronger formal constraints disappear.

## Limitation Or Open Question
The paper shows that ambiguity can be efficient under informative context, but it does not establish that all major properties of language are explained this way. The open question is how far communicative efficiency can reach before richer structural, acquisition, or cognitive constraints need to do explanatory work.
